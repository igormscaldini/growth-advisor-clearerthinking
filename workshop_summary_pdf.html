Spencer's #1 Self-Help Technique

Why this workshop exists

For his forthcoming book The 12 Levers (co-authored with Jeremy Stevenson), Spencer studied more than 100 self-improvement books and over 20 types of therapy, extracting nearly 500 distinct techniques into a single database. The guiding question: What are all the psychological strategies that really work to improve your life?

The surprising answer: almost everything boils down to just 12 high-level psychological strategies — the "levers." This workshop focused on Lever 1: unraveling the causes (fittingly, its icon is a magnifying glass) — the technique Spencer picked when asked, "If you could recommend only one approach to improve people's lives, what would it be?"

His answer: it's very hard to solve a problem you don't deeply understand. Most of us understand our problems only at the surface level, and going deeper unlocks far better solutions.

The core idea: surface vs. deep understanding

The broken-down car. If you're not a car expert and your car won't start, you start guessing — check the gas, replace the battery, throw random fixes at it. You can waste months and a lot of money. An expert runs diagnostics, finds the real cause, and fixes it in one stroke. Your life problems often work the same way.

The "Five Whys." A technique borrowed from business: keep asking "why" to move past the surface cause to the deeper one.

"Why do we keep fighting?" → She wants X, I want Y.
"Why does that lead to fighting instead of a compromise?" → We tried to make a plan once and couldn't agree.
"Why couldn't we agree?" → She thought I was being selfish.

Two couples can face the identical disagreement — one fights endlessly, the other calmly works out a balance. The disagreement isn't the real cause. Keep digging.

1. Look for alternate causes

We often latch onto a cause too quickly — and anchor on the wrong one. Three stories show the danger:

  • Depression that was actually anxiety. A friend was convinced he was depressed. His symptoms sounded more like anxiety, so he took standardized questionnaires for both — and scored low on depression, high on anxiety. The problem wasn't solved instantly, but it was completely reframed.
  • The mysterious cough (Spencer's own story). After moving into a new home, Spencer developed a cough that worsened for weeks until he struggled to breathe. Doctors found nothing; environmental fixes did nothing. Almost as an afterthought, a mold inspector ran an air test and found a large patch of mold hidden behind the wall, three feet from where he slept. He has a mold allergy. Once it was removed, he recovered in three weeks.
  • Carrie Poppy's "haunting." She felt a wrongness, heard whooshing sounds, and feared she was cursed or going insane. A skeptically minded group asked: could it be carbon monoxide? It was — a gas leak was poisoning her. The wrong explanation could have cost her life.
Try it
Write down three challenges you're facing (mental health, relationships, work — anything with a psychological element). For each, spend about 20 seconds asking: Could there be a different cause than the one I've anchored on?

2. Emotions as a source of information

Think of each emotion as a smoke detector — except each one detects something different: fear detects danger, disgust detects contamination, sadness detects loss.

Like a smoke detector, the useful question is: is it working properly? An alarm that goes off every time you cook isn't helping. Your emotions carry real information — so when one fires, it's worth asking what it's responding to.

Three types of emotional response

  • Proportionate — your dog dies and you feel deep sadness. The emotion matches the situation.
  • Useful but not proportionate — a creepy dark alley makes you leave. The actual risk may be low, but the instinct is still a good strategy.
  • Dysregulated — neither proportionate nor useful, such as flying into anger over something minor in a way that damages your relationships.

3. The "Inner Why"

You have thousands of fleeting thoughts a day and remember almost none. So when an emotion shifts, the triggering thought is often already lost — leaving you saying "I felt stressed at work but I don't know why."

The method: notice the moment your emotion changes, and immediately ask why — as close to real time as possible. The faster you catch it, the more likely you'll identify the true trigger.

Example

You watch for the moment stress hits at work. You arrive — no stress. You meet your boss — no stress. You bump into a particular colleague — stress spikes. Now you've learned something concrete to explore.

Try it
Where in your life could you apply the Inner Why — noticing an emotional shift and asking why? (Participants offered: avoiding people, resisting decluttering, sudden anger at a child who isn't listening.)

4. The ABC Analysis

A structured version of the Inner Why, drawn from cognitive behavioral therapy. It works best for recurring situations where things reliably go badly or you feel upset.

  • A — Activating event: what actually happens right before things go wrong?
  • B — Belief / interpretation: the thoughts, interpretations, or judgments running through your mind in that moment.
  • C — Consequences: how you feel and how you act — plus what results.
Example — getting angry at your child

A: You ask your child to do something and they seem to completely ignore you.
B: "They're purposely ignoring me." / "They never listen to me."
C: You feel angry → you start yelling → your child gets upset → now everyone is upset.

The key insight: between an event and your reaction sits a layer of interpretation — often so fast you don't notice it. Those interpretations may be inaccurate, or even if accurate, unhelpful. Mapping the chain lets you see and question them.

Try it
Pick a recurring situation that goes badly. Write out its A, B, and C in detail.

5. The Bright Spot Analysis

This one is especially powerful for problems that feel intractable — "I'm always depressed," "I hate my job," "I can never make friends." These feel like flat, unchanging facts with nowhere to start.

The move: look for the variation. Surely some days are less bad than others. Zoom in on those.

  • On the days you felt less depressed — what was different? Exercise, a walk outside, time with family, a creative project?
  • When did making a friendship come closest to working?
  • When does work go smoother than usual?

Any variation — even within something that feels constantly bad — is a clue to the underlying causes you may have missed. Days that go better than expected point you toward what actually helps.

Try it
Find a challenge where this applies. Ask: When does it go better than normal, and what's different on those occasions? If nothing comes to mind, start noticing it going forward.

The one principle to take away

Better understanding leads to better solutions — and you have many tools to reach that understanding.
