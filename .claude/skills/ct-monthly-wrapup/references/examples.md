## JUNE 2026 (sent July 2026) — full

**Some of Our Most Important Ideas From June, In 2 Minutes**

This month, we looked at a few things that people often feel confident about: therapy, charity, IQ, self-help, and evidence. In each case, the answer was a bit more complicated than it first seemed. Some advice works only if it gives you something concrete to do. Some evidence is much stronger than other evidence. And some things that seem important, like IQ, matter in some ways but not in others.

A few ideas worth keeping:

- **Painful thoughts are often persuasive, not necessarily accurate.** One of CBT's central moves is not "think positively," but look for a thought that is at least as true and more useful than the one currently derailing you. Depressed or anxious moods tend to filter attention toward confirming evidence. Asking, "What evidence does not support this thought?" can reopen access to information your brain is temporarily hiding from itself. (🎧 Podcast: When painful thoughts feel true but aren't)
- **Optimization can quietly punish ambition.** If you only optimize for what can be measured with confidence, you may favor small, legible gains over harder-to-measure interventions with much larger upside. This does not mean abandoning models. It means noticing when "precision" is really just excluding messy second-order effects, scale, political change, or implementation quality because they are hard to quantify. (🎧 Podcast: Is cash a better form of charitable aid?)
- **Evidence does not remove judgment.** Even strong evidence usually tells us what tends to happen, not what we should value. A treatment can work and still be wrong for a patient. A policy can be effective and still raise cost, fairness, or liberty questions. Evidence is a crucial input, but pretending it can make the whole decision for us often hides the value judgments rather than eliminating them. (🎧 Podcast: The Hidden History of Evidence-Based Everything)
- **Good self-help needs instructions, not incantations.** "Believe in yourself" and "stop comparing yourself to others" may be decent destinations, but they are poor directions. A useful technique should tell you what to actually do with your attention, thoughts, body, or speech. Otherwise, it is like IKEA instructions that say, "Become a bookshelf." (🎧 Podcast: The Twelve Levers for a better life)
- **Not all evidence earns the same weight.** Anecdotes, animal studies, observational studies, randomized trials, and meta-analyses are not interchangeable. The beta-carotene story is a useful warning: observational studies suggested it protected against lung cancer, but randomized trials found supplements increased cancer rates. Confounders can make a false causal story look elegant, especially when the mechanism sounds plausible. (📝 Article: How to Tell Strong Evidence from Weak Evidence)
- **IQ predicts some things, but not the thing everyone wants.** IQ is scientifically meaningful: it correlates with many cognitive tasks and some life outcomes. But in our data, it did not predict happiness or life satisfaction. That is a useful corrective to both extremes: IQ is not meaningless, but it is also not wisdom, character, discipline, social skill, or a reliable route to feeling okay inside your own life. (📝 Article: The Strangest Things that Correlate with IQ)
- **You can improve performance without raising IQ.** Cognitive ability matters, but practice with good feedback can dominate it for specific skills. Someone who has studied math carefully for ten years will usually outperform someone slightly higher-IQ who has not studied math. For most things you care about, the practical question is not "How do I become generally smarter?" but "How do I practice this well?" (▶️ Video: The Strangest Things that Correlate with IQ)

**A small experiment for this week:**

Pick one recurring problem in your life: procrastinating, bickering, doomscrolling, skipping sleep, avoiding a hard conversation. Write a tiny plan with three parts: (1) risk factors, (2) early warning signs, and (3) what you will do when it starts. The point is not to become a flawless productivity monk. It is to stop meeting the same problem each week with only vibes and mild regret.

## MAY 2026 (sent June 2026) — full

**Some of Our Most Important Ideas From May, In 2 Minutes**

This month kept circling one awkward fact: progress rarely arrives wearing a name tag. It shows up as a chatbot that is suddenly useful, a "new" self-help trick with an ancient passport, a personality model that explains something but not everything, or a social norm still charging rent after it was supposedly evicted. If your inbox has also achieved artificial general clutter, here is the compressed version. The theme, roughly: beware clean stories about messy humans.

Here are a few of the useful ideas from our content that was published in May:

- **Self-help has fewer core ideas than bookstore shelves imply.** Many techniques (even extremely useful ones!) are recycled or repackaged under different names: mindfulness, exposure, reframing, acceptance, and so on. That is not necessarily bad, but it can create the illusion of endless novelty. The enthusiasm also often outstrips the evidence. A useful mindset is to treat techniques as hypotheses to test, not as verdicts about what will work for you. (▶️ Video: I read 100 self-improvement books. This is what they said.)
- **AI is both overhyped and under-absorbed.** One reason people are critical of AI is that it still makes mistakes, but this criticism can miss an important point. The striking thing is not that current systems are flawless, but that they have rapidly become useful across math, coding, medicine, research, images, video, music, and more. There are still valid criticisms, but a better posture might be neither worship nor sneering: if you're not ethically opposed to doing so, experiment to find where it genuinely improves work, avoid slop, and keep a deliberate news diet. (▶️ Video: We need to talk about AI…)
- **Academic papers can be hard to find, but there are helpful techniques you can learn.** Media claims about research often oversimplify the original evidence so much that important caveats disappear. Learning to find full texts through resources like Google Scholar, author websites, browser extensions, research tools, or polite emails gives you better access to the truth. AI can help explain dense papers, but should be treated as a co-reader, not an oracle. (📝 Article: Why You Should Know How to Find Academic Papers, and 7 Ways to do so)
- **When making things, don't confuse value and interestingness.** If you want your work to have an impact, avoid the common trap of thinking that the mere fact that something is interesting to you means that it will be valuable to others. Impact is partly value per person ✕ people reached. Both halves of that equation deserve consideration, but the first one is often misunderstood or neglected because it is less glamorous than the second. (📝 Article: How to create valuable things that people actually want to use)
- **Five numbers capture a lot about your personality, and adding a sixth doesn't necessarily help.** The 'Big Five' and 'HEXACO' personality models both compress messy human differences. In our study, HEXACO's sixth factor, Honesty-Humility, seemed useful in some contexts, but only added a small predictive gain overall. Five factors looked like a reasonable stopping point: not because humans are simple, but because every compact model has to trade detail for usefulness. (📝 Article: How Many Traits Make Up Your Personality?)
- **Overconfidence has favorite habitats.** People tend to overrate themselves most on skills that are easy, subjective, identity-relevant, and familiar. This helps explain why people are more prone to self-flattery about more subjective or vaguely-defined traits like "intelligence". Race-car driving feels hard and measurable, so people are more modest. Everyday driving, by contrast, gives room to define "good" in whatever way makes one look competent. (▶️ Video: Psychology researcher answers Reddit's most controversial psychology questions)
- **Therapy should have a job description.** Long therapy is not automatically a problem; years without progress might be. A useful test is whether you and your therapist have concrete goals, whether you can tell if things are changing, and whether you are willing to try different methods, providers, or formats if nothing moves. A good therapist may eventually help you graduate from their therapy altogether. (▶️ Video: Psychology researcher answers Reddit's most controversial psychology questions)
- **The next AI question may be social, not technical.** If leading AI companies are genuinely trying to build superintelligence, the hardest problem may not be whether capability arrives, but whether anyone can coordinate around restraint. A treaty would likely need to start with concrete mutual interests, such as limiting dangerous cyber or bio capabilities, chip tracking, and verification. The "doomer" frame can obscure the optimistic claim: societies sometimes do choose sanity. (🎧 Podcast: Could an international agreement protect us from dangerous AI? with Malo Bourgon)
- **Gender roles can hurt everyone (regardless of gender).** Kate Manne came on the Clearer Thinking podcast and discussed the ways they can hurt people, as well as her ideal for the future: A society in which people, regardless of gender or sexuality, are free to flourish and be fully themselves, unconstrained by gender roles. (🎧 Podcast: Is patriarchy gone or hiding in plain sight? with Kate Manne)

**May's Actionable Insight: Networking by doing a 5-minute favor**

Pick someone you recently met or want to stay connected with, then send a useful introduction, article, book, tool, or note that addresses something they actually care about. No "we should catch up sometime" fog machine required. Done well, this is networking with less cologne and more usefulness.
