## Some of Our Most Important Ideas From June, In 2 Minutes

[header banner — assets/image2.jpg]

This month we looked at a few things people often feel sure about: intelligence, evidence, self-help, and how much any of them really shape a good life. In most cases the honest answer turned out to be a bit more complicated than it first seems. Here is a quick tour of what we published in June.

A few ideas worth keeping:

**IQ predicts real things, just not the one everyone wants.** IQ is scientifically meaningful: it correlates with many cognitive tasks, and in our data higher-IQ people were even a bit better at spotting pseudo-profound nonsense. But in our study of over 3,000 people, it showed no link to happiness or life satisfaction, and little to no link to conscientiousness or diligence. That might be a useful corrective to both camps: IQ isn't meaningless, but it probably isn't wisdom, character, or a reliable route to feeling okay inside your own life. (📝 Article: [The Strangest Things that Correlate with IQ](https://www.clearerthinking.org/post/the-strangest-things-that-correlate-with-iq))

**You can probably get better at almost anything without raising your IQ.** Cognitive ability matters, but practice with fast, reliable feedback seems to dominate it for any specific skill. Someone who has studied math carefully for ten years will usually outperform someone ten IQ points higher who hasn't studied it. So the more practical question may be less "How do I get smarter?" and more "How do I practice this well?" (▶️ Video: [The Strangest Things that Correlate with IQ](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yk4XMhqwz6E))

**Not all evidence deserves equal weight.** Anecdotes, animal studies, observational studies, randomized trials, and meta-analyses aren't interchangeable. The beta-carotene story is a useful cautionary tale: observational studies suggested it protected against lung cancer, but randomized trials found that supplements actually increased cancer rates, and one large trial was stopped early over "substantial evidence of possible harm." A confounder, here the healthier habits of people who tend to eat their vegetables, can make a false causal story look elegant. (📝 Article: [How to Tell Strong Evidence from Weak Evidence](https://www.clearerthinking.org/post/how-to-tell-strong-evidence-from-weak-evidence))

**Evidence can tell you what works, but not what to value.** Synthesizing evidence may matter as much as collecting it: because early studies weren't pooled, decades of advice to put babies face-down persisted, and by one estimate around 50,000 deaths might have been prevented. Yet even strong, well-synthesized evidence tends to tell you what usually happens, not whether the cost, the trade-offs, or a particular patient's preferences make it the right call. And openly admitting what we don't yet know may build more trust than confident pronouncements. (🎧 Podcast: [The Hidden History of Evidence-Based Everything, with Helen Pearson](https://podcast.clearerthinking.org/episode/315/helen-pearson-the-hidden-history-of-evidence-based-everything))

**Good self-help tends to give you steps, not slogans.** "Love yourself" and "think positive" often fail not because they're wrong but because you can't really execute them. The techniques that seem to work operate on the four things you can actually control: your attention, thoughts, body, and speech. The aim is more like building a toolkit than finding a silver bullet, ideally practiced in the "stretch zone" between boredom and panic, since no single method works for every person or every season of life. (🎧 Podcast: [The Twelve Levers for a better life, with Jeremy Stevenson](https://podcast.clearerthinking.org/episode/316/jeremy-stevenson-the-twelve-levers-for-a-better-life))

**It's often better to focus on the current cause than the original one.** It's natural to want to know why a problem started, whether it's the childhood, the crash, or the breakup. But knowing the origin rarely dissolves the symptom on its own. What keeps you anxious or stuck now, whether a recurring thought, a habit of isolating, or a difficult environment, is usually both the real driver and the part you can actually change. You can't change what happened in the past, but you can usually change what is happening in the present. (📝 Article: [Four Overlooked and Important Ideas We Found by Studying 106 Self-Help Books](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/clearer-thinking-today/202606/self-help-advices-people-keep-overlooking))

**Small actions may rewrite identity more reliably than big breakthroughs.** Lasting change often seems to come from repeated, low-resistance actions rather than a single breakthrough. A relapse might be better read as a learning signal than a moral verdict, and it can help to separate what you desire in the moment from what you actually value, aiming for the 'middle way' rather than all-or-nothing effort. (🎧 Podcast: [How Small Actions Rewrite Identity, with Eric Zimmer](https://podcast.clearerthinking.org/episode/317/eric-zimmer-how-small-actions-rewrite-identity))

**Many disagreements may really be clashes of whole theories.** People often disagree not because they know different facts, but because they hold different underlying theories of how society works. Our new free tool maps over 100 influential theories, from mainstream to fringe, as visual flow charts, so you can explore your favorite thinkers more deeply, meet ideas you've never encountered, and get a better sense of the worldview of people you disagree with. (👤 New Tool: [Society Explained](https://society.clearerthinking.org/))

## June's Actionable Insight: Write a plan for a recurring problem

Pick one problem that keeps showing up in your life: bickering with your partner, doomscrolling, procrastinating, skipping sleep, or dodging a hard conversation. Then write a tiny plan with three parts: (1) the **risk factors** that make it more likely, (2) the **early warning signs** that it's starting, and (3) exactly **what you'll do** when it does. The point isn't to become a flawless productivity monk. It's to stop meeting the same problem each week with only vibes and mild regret. This isn't just self-help folklore: a 2021 meta-analysis found that people with written safety plans were 43% less likely to attempt suicide.

From 📝 Article: [Four Overlooked and Important Ideas We Found by Studying 106 Self-Help Books](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/clearer-thinking-today/202606/self-help-advices-people-keep-overlooking)
