# Why copy reads as generic or machine-written, and what to do instead

Igor named this as the problem to fix (2026-11-27). The exact words and patterns that can be matched mechanically live in `scripts/copy_lint.py`, which is their single source; this file explains the families, so that you can recognize a new variant the checker does not know and fix the cause instead of swapping one stock phrase for another.

The root cause is nearly always the same: the sentence was written from the *category* (a personality test, a newsletter, a productivity tool) instead of from the *case* (this test, this finding, this reader). The fix is nearly always the same too: go back to the ledger of specifics and say the particular thing.

## 1. Sentences that fit anywhere

"A powerful tool to help you grow." "Insights you can use in everyday life." Nothing in them is false, and nothing in them is ours.

Test: paste the sentence into a competitor's page in your head. If it still works there, it carries no information here.

Instead: the number, the mechanism, what the reader sees on the screen, how long it takes, what it is based on.

## 2. Abstractions where a thing should be

"Improve your decision-making", "enhance your wellbeing", "gain clarity". The reader cannot picture any of it.

Instead: the situation. "The next time you are choosing between two job offers." "On a Sunday night when the week already feels too full."

## 3. Reflex shapes

These are sentence shapes that language models (and tired copywriters) reach for regardless of content. A reader who has seen a few hundred of them recognizes the shape before reading the words.

- **Contrast with a claim nobody made.** "It's not just a quiz, it's a journey." "This isn't about willpower. It's about design." The first half exists only to be knocked down. Say the positive point alone. (A real misconception that readers actually hold is different: then name it and correct it with evidence.)
- **The reflex list of three.** "Clear, practical, and evidence-based." Three because three sounds finished, not because there are three things. Keep a triad only when there are exactly three items and each is specific (three named frameworks, three real steps).
- **The staged reveal.** "The result? ..." "Here's the thing: ..." "The truth is ..." A drum roll before an ordinary sentence. Say the sentence.
- **The stock opener.** "Ever wondered why ...?" "Imagine ..." "In today's fast-paced world ..." "We've all been there." Open with the finding, the reader's problem in their words, or the ask.
- **The stock closer.** A last line that restates the piece as a slogan ("Because clearer thinking starts with a single question."), or "Ready to ...?" End on the action or the most useful fact, and stop.
- **Staccato fragments.** "No fluff. No jargon. Just science." One sentence can say it, if it needs saying.
- **The audience cover.** "Whether you're a student or a CEO ..." It tries to include everyone and so speaks to no one. Choose the reader.
- **The question the next sentence answers.** "So what does this mean for you? It means ..." Cut the question.

## 4. Borrowed feeling

Intensifiers and sincerity markers ("truly", "genuinely", "incredibly"), exclamation marks, stock reassurance ("you're not alone", "it's okay to feel this way"), hype ("game-changing", "life-changing"). They tell the reader how to feel instead of giving them a reason to feel it.

Instead: the fact that would produce the feeling. If the sentence is true without the adverb, cut the adverb. For a reader in distress, be specific about their situation and offer one small step; that is warmer than any stock phrase.

## 5. Borrowed authority

"Studies show", "experts agree", "science-backed", a quotation from Aristotle or Einstein. Authority with no address.

Instead: the study, named or linked, with what it measured. Or no claim.

## 6. Uniform rhythm and symmetrical structure

Every sentence the same length. Every paragraph three sentences. Every bullet a bold two-word label, a colon and one clause. Every section ending on a one-line moral. Each choice is fine alone; the regularity is what gives it away.

Instead: let the content set the shape. A point that needs one sentence gets one. A list with two items is a sentence, not bullets. Vary on purpose, and put the thing that matters in a short sentence after a longer one.

## 7. The hedge stack and the both-sides shrug

"This can often help to potentially improve ..." "There are many factors to consider." Hedging every verb is not caution; it hides what we actually know.

Instead: one plain statement of what was found and one plain statement of its limit.

## 8. Saying it twice

A sentence, then the same sentence in other words ("In other words ..."), then a summary of both. Or an introduction that announces what the piece will say.

Instead: trust the reader. Say it once, well.

## 9. Formatting tells

Emoji at the start of every bullet, Title Case On Every Heading, bold scattered through a paragraph, headers on a piece of 100 words, em dashes. In CT copy: sentence case, bold only for what the reader must not miss, emoji only where a format calls for one (one in an Instagram hook line, the sponsor-box headline), and no em dashes at all.

## 10. The vocabulary

Words that have become markers through overuse: delve, unlock, elevate, empower, journey, landscape, tapestry, seamless, leverage, and their relatives. The full list is in the checker. Replacing one with a synonym does not help; the sentence is usually a category sentence (family 1) and needs a fact, not a different verb.

## A last check

Read the piece and ask of each paragraph: *what does the reader know now that they did not know before it?* If the answer is nothing, the paragraph is generic however clean the checker's report is.
