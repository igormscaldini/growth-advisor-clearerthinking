## 1. The calibration sample

On 2026-11-27 Igor was shown the same passage at three settings of "poetry" and chose the middle one. It is the reference for how much lyricism CT copy carries.

**Chosen: one beat of meaning, then the point.**

> Every plan is a small work of fiction, and we are generous authors. When psychology students predicted how long their theses would take, they said 34 days on average. They took 56.
>
> The error has a name, the planning fallacy, and a cause: we picture the project going well and forget how the last ones went.
>
> So borrow from your own history. Before your next project, ask how long the last three like it took. Use that number, not your gut.

Why it works, sentence by sentence:

- *The beat* ("a small work of fiction"): one image, built from plain concrete words. A skeptic accepts it, because a plan really is a story about events that have not happened.
- *The evidence arrives at once*: the next sentence is a study, with who was asked, what was measured, and two numbers. The beat is cashed out before it can float away.
- *The short sentence carries the weight*: "They took 56." Four words after a long sentence.
- *Name and mechanism*: the term appears once, together with what it means and why it happens.
- *The action is small and specific*: one question, a number to use, something to do before the next project.
- *No adjectives are doing the persuading.* Nothing is "powerful", "surprising" or "fascinating". The gap between 34 and 56 does that work.

(Source: Buehler, Griffin and Ross, 1994, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 67(3), study 1: 37 students, predicted 33.9 days, actual 55.5, about 30% finished by their predicted date. Figures checked against secondary summaries on 2026-11-27, not against the paper's results section; check the paper before publishing them.)

**Rejected as too plain** (no image at all; correct, but nothing to remember):

> Psychology students were asked to predict when they would finish their theses. On average they said 34 days. They took 56. Fewer than a third finished on time.

This register is still right in places where a beat does not belong: buttons, instructions, assessment questions, error messages, email gates.

**Rejected as too literary** (the image runs through the whole piece and the action arrives late):

> We live a little ahead of ourselves. The thesis is already finished in the mind, the house already painted, and the future always looks tidier than the past ever managed to be. [...] They call it the planning fallacy. I think of it as optimism with no memory. The remedy is to give hope a memory.

Three images in a row and a metaphor carried to the last line turn a finding into a mood. One beat, then facts.

## 2. The six habits, worked

### Start from something only this piece could say

| Could be anyone's | Only ours |
|---|---|
| "A powerful personality test to help you understand yourself." | "Which of 16 personality types fits you best? One test gives you your Big Five traits, your Enneagram type and your Jungian type." |
| "We put astrology to the test, and the results are surprising." | "We asked 152 astrologers to match 12 people to their birth charts. The best score was 5 of 12." |

The right-hand column cannot be pasted onto another site. That is the whole test.

### Show the reasoning

Assertion: "This tool helps you beat procrastination."

Reasoned: "Unstuck asks what is in the way (anxiety, low motivation, tiredness or trouble focusing) and gives you one short technique for that. Then you pick a step small enough to start, and a 10-minute timer runs while you do it."

The second version makes no promise about results. It shows the logic of the tool and lets the reader judge whether that logic fits their problem. That is "explain, do not persuade" in practice, and it is also more convincing.

### Be exactly as confident as the evidence

See section 4.

### Plain words for a smart outsider

- Prefer the word a person would say across a table: "use", not "utilize"; "about", not "approximately"; "shows", not "demonstrates".
- One idea per sentence. If a sentence needs two commas and a semicolon, it is two sentences.
- A term of art appears once, with its meaning attached: "the planning fallacy: we picture the project going well and forget how the last ones went."
- Numbers beat adjectives. "Takes about 10 minutes" beats "quick".

### Make it usable

A good ask has four parts, and most of them fit in one sentence: what to do, how long it takes, what you get, what it costs. "Take the free test (about 15 minutes) and get a report on your 16-type match, Big Five traits and Enneagram type." Leave out any part you cannot verify rather than guessing it.

## 3. The lyrical beat and the woo test

A beat is one sentence, occasionally two, where the copy steps back from the facts and says something true about being a person. It gives the reader a reason to care before the reasons to act. It is the "slightly poetic" in Igor's brief, and the word "slightly" matters.

**What a good beat is made of**

- Concrete nouns and plain verbs: plan, fiction, author; sky, chart; task, week.
- A truth the reader recognizes from their own life, said in a way they have not heard.
- A direct line to the evidence or the mechanism that follows it.

**The woo test.** A beat stays only if all four answers are yes:

1. *Would a skeptic accept it as true?* Literally, or as an obvious figure of speech. "Every plan is a small work of fiction": yes. "Your mind already knows the answer": no.
2. *Is it free of borrowed vocabulary?* Nothing from mysticism (universe, energy, soul, destiny, manifest, sacred) and nothing from therapy-speak or self-help (journey, authentic self, healing, inner wisdom, hold space, best self).
3. *Are concrete things doing the work?* Abstractions as actors ("growth invites", "clarity emerges", "your potential awaits") fail.
4. *Does the next sentence or two cash it out?* A fact, a number or a mechanism must follow. A beat with nothing behind it is decoration.

**Where the beat comes from.** Three reliable sources, in order of safety:

1. *The reader's own situation*, stated plainly. For a procrastination tool: "You already know what the task is. You have probably known for days." No research claim, nothing to doubt, and the reader feels seen.
2. *The finding, compressed into an image.* The calibration sample does this.
3. *The long view.* "For thousands of years people have looked at the sky and found their own lives written there." Use sparingly: it is the easiest kind to overdo.

**Where it goes**

| Format | Beat? |
|---|---|
| Page | The headline or the first line under it, not both. A long page may carry a second one near the close. |
| Tool intro screen, results framing | One, if it helps the reader care. |
| Assessment questions, instructions, buttons, labels, errors, email gates | Never. These must be literal. |
| Ads | Rarely. A short headline has room for a clear promise or a beat, and the promise wins. A beat can open the longer text. |
| Social caption | The hook line is either the beat or the finding. Do not stack both before the reader knows the topic. |

**What a beat is not:** a pun, a joke, a quotation from a famous person, a rhetorical question, or a closing slogan. And it never delays the ask past the second paragraph.

## 4. Confidence and evidence

"Scientific and confident" does not mean certain. It means the reader can see exactly how much we know.

| Overclaim | Mush | CT |
|---|---|---|
| "Scientifically proven to end procrastination." | "This may potentially help some people feel somewhat more productive." | "In one study, students predicted their theses would take 34 days. They took 56." |
| "Science says astrology is fake." | "The evidence on astrology is arguably mixed." | "152 astrologers tried to match 12 people to their charts. The best score was 5 of 12." |

Rules of thumb:

- **Mechanisms are free, outcomes cost evidence.** "The tool asks you to rate each option on the things you care about" needs no citation. "The tool improves your decisions" does. When there is no evidence for an outcome, describe the mechanism and stop.
- **One good number beats three adjectives.** Give the number its unit and its denominator ("5 of 12", "about a third of 37 students").
- **State the limit when a careful reader would ask.** "In one study", "among students", "self-reported". One clause is enough. A limit stated plainly reads as confidence; a hedge on every verb reads as fear.
- **Never write "studies show" without the study.** Name it in the sentence, or link the relevant words to the DOI, or drop the claim.
- **Sizes have names.** For correlations, CT uses Cohen's rubric (small, medium, large). Do not call an effect "huge" or "dramatic".
- **Do not prove, measure.** "Proven" is not a word science uses about itself.

## 5. Before and after, by format

### Page (first screen of a tool page)

Before:

> **Unlock Your Productivity Potential**
> Unstuck is a powerful, science-backed tool that empowers you to overcome procrastination and achieve your goals. Whether you're a student or a busy professional, it's time to take control. Ready to get started?

After:

> **Stuck on a task? Start with ten minutes.**
> You already know what the task is. You have probably known for days.
>
> Unstuck asks what is in the way (anxiety, low motivation, tiredness or trouble focusing) and gives you one short technique for that. Then you choose a step small enough to start, and a 10-minute timer runs while you do it.
>
> [Start a 10-minute round]

What changed: the headline is the ask. The beat comes from the reader's situation and makes no research claim. The body describes what the tool does and promises nothing it cannot back. The button names the action.

### In-tool text (email gate before results)

Before:

> 🎉 Congratulations! Your personalized results are ready! Enter your email to unlock them and join our community of curious minds!

After:

> **Your results are ready.**
> Enter your email to see them. We will also send you [fact needed: which newsletter and how often], and you can unsubscribe at any time.
>
> [See my results]

What changed: no beat, because a gate is a transaction and the reader wants to know exactly what they are agreeing to. The missing fact is marked instead of invented, and no privacy promise is made that nobody has verified.

### Ad (search headline and description)

Before:

> Discover Your True Self Today
> Unlock the secrets of your personality with our revolutionary test!

After:

> Which of 16 Types Fits You Best?
> One free test, three frameworks: your Big Five traits, Enneagram type and Jungian type.

What changed: a question the reader actually has, answered by the specifics of the product. The list of three stays because there are exactly three frameworks.

### Social caption

Before:

> 🚀 Excited to share our latest study! In today's world, astrology is more popular than ever. But does it really work? The results might surprise you. 👇

After:

> For thousands of years people have looked at the sky and found their own lives written there. In 2024 we asked 152 astrologers to do it under test conditions: match 12 people to their birth charts.
>
> The best score was 5 of 12.
>
> How the test worked, and what it does and does not show: [link]

What changed: the beat is the hook, the finding follows immediately and gets a line to itself, and the last line promises the method and the limits, which is what a skeptical reader wants before clicking.
