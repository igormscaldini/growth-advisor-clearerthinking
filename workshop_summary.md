# Understanding Your Problems Deeply: A Workshop Summary

*A written recap of the Clearer Thinking workshop led by Spencer Greenberg, exploring the single most useful psychological approach to improving your life — drawn from research for the upcoming book* The 12 Levers.

---

## Why this workshop exists

For his forthcoming book *The 12 Levers* (co-authored with Jeremy Stevenson), Spencer studied more than 100 self-improvement books and over 20 types of therapy, extracting nearly 500 distinct techniques into a single database. The guiding question: *What are all the psychological strategies you can use to improve your life?*

The surprising takeaway was that almost everything boils down to just **12 high-level psychological strategies** — the "levers." This workshop focuses on **Lever 1: unraveling the causes** (the magnifying glass), which Spencer picked when asked, "If you could recommend only one approach to improve people's lives, what would it be?"

His answer: **It's very hard to solve a problem you don't deeply understand.** Most of us understand our problems only at the surface level, and a deeper understanding can unlock far better solutions.

---

## The core idea: surface understanding vs. deep understanding

**The broken-down car.** If you're not a car expert and your car won't start, you start guessing — check the gas, replace the battery, throw random fixes at it. You can waste months and thousands of dollars. An expert runs diagnostics, finds the real cause, and fixes it in one stroke. Our life problems work the same way.

**The "Five Whys."** A technique borrowed from business: keep asking "why" to move past the surface cause to the deeper one.

> *"Why do we keep fighting?"* → "She wants X, I want Y."
> *"Why does that lead to fighting instead of a compromise?"* → "We tried to make a plan once and couldn't agree."
> *"Why couldn't we agree?"* → "She thought I was being selfish."

Two couples can face the identical disagreement (one wants X, one wants Y) — one fights endlessly, the other calmly works out a balance. The disagreement isn't the real cause. Keep digging.

## Technique 1: Look for alternate causes

We often latch onto a cause too quickly — and anchor on the wrong one. Three stories illustrate the danger:

- **Depression that was actually anxiety.** A friend was convinced he was depressed. As they talked, his symptoms sounded more like anxiety. He took a standardized depression questionnaire and an anxiety questionnaire — and scored low on depression, high on anxiety. The problem wasn't solved instantly, but it was completely reframed.

- **The mysterious cough (Spencer's own story).** After moving into a new home, Spencer developed a cough that worsened for weeks until he struggled to breathe. Doctors found nothing. An environmental inspector fixed three issues — no change. Almost as an afterthought, a mold inspector ran an air test and found a large patch of mold hidden behind the wall, three feet from where he slept. He has a mold allergy. Removed it; recovered in three weeks.

- **Carrie Poppy's "haunting."** She felt a wrongness, heard whooshing sounds, and feared she was cursed or going insane. A skeptically-minded ghost-hunting group asked: *could it be carbon monoxide?* It was — a gas leak was poisoning her. The wrong explanation (spiritual or psychiatric) could have cost her life.

**Common real-world alternate causes worth checking:**
- **Sleep apnea** can cause depression, low energy, and brain fog — and many people don't know they have it.
- **Anxiety** can cause stomach and digestive problems, which are easy to treat as purely physical.

> **Exercise:** Write down three challenges you're facing (mental health, relationships, work — anything with a psychological element). For each, spend ~20 seconds asking: *Could there be a different cause than the one I've anchored on?*

## Technique 2: Emotions as a source of information

Think of each emotion as a **smoke detector** — except each one detects something different:

- **Fear** detects danger.
- **Disgust** detects risk of contamination (illness, bad food, infection).
- **Sadness** detects loss.

Like a smoke detector, the useful question is: *Is it working properly?* An alarm that goes off every time you cook isn't helping. Emotions carry real information about what's going on — so when one fires, it's worth asking what it's responding to.

**Three types of emotional response:**
1. **Proportionate** — your dog dies and you feel deep sadness. The emotion matches the situation.
2. **Useful but not proportionate** — a creepy dark alley makes you afraid and you leave. The actual risk may be low, but the instinct to avoid it is still a good strategy.
3. **Dysregulated** — neither proportionate nor useful. E.g., flying into anger over something minor in a way that damages your relationships.

---

## Technique 3: The "Inner Why"

We have thousands of fleeting thoughts a day and remember almost none. So when an emotion shifts, the triggering thought is often already lost — leaving us saying *"I felt stressed at work but I don't know why."*

**The method:** Notice the *moment* your emotion changes, and immediately ask **why** — as close to real time as possible. The faster you catch it, the more likely you'll identify the true trigger.

> **Example:** You commit to watching for the moment stress hits at work. You arrive — no stress. You meet your boss — no stress. You bump into a particular colleague — *stress spikes.* Now you've learned something concrete to explore.

> **Exercise:** Where in your life could you apply the Inner Why — noticing an emotional shift and asking why? (Workshop participants offered: avoiding people, resisting decluttering, sudden anger at a child who isn't listening.)

---

## Technique 4: The ABC Analysis

A structured version of the Inner Why, drawn from cognitive behavioral therapy. Best used for **recurring situations** where things reliably go badly or you feel upset.

- **A — Activating event:** What actually happens right before things go wrong?
- **B — Belief / interpretation:** The thoughts, interpretations, or judgments running through your mind in that moment.
- **C — Consequences:** How you feel (emotional) and how you act (behavioral), plus what results.

> **Example — getting angry at your child:**
> - **A:** You ask your child to do something and they seem to completely ignore you.
> - **B:** *"They're purposely ignoring me."* / *"They never listen to me."*
> - **C:** You feel angry → you start yelling → your child gets upset → now everyone is upset.

The key insight: between an event and our reaction sits a layer of interpretation — often so fast we don't notice it. Those interpretations may be **inaccurate**, or even if accurate, **unhelpful**. Mapping the chain lets you see and question them.

> **Exercise:** Pick a recurring situation that goes badly. Write out its A, B, and C in detail.

---

## Technique 5: The Bright Spot Analysis

Especially powerful for problems that feel **intractable** — "I'm always depressed," "I hate my job," "I can never make friends." These feel like flat, unchanging facts with nowhere to start.

**The move:** Look for the variation. Surely *some* days are less bad than others. Zoom in on those.

- On the days you felt *less* depressed — what was different? Did you exercise, walk outside, see family, work on a creative project?
- When did making a friendship come *closest* to working?
- When does work go *smoother* than usual?

Any variation — even within something that's "always bad" — is a clue to the underlying causes you may have missed. Days that go better than expected point you toward what actually helps.

> **Exercise:** Find a challenge where this applies. Ask: *When does it go better than normal, and what's different on those occasions?* If nothing comes to mind, start noticing it going forward.

---

## The one principle to take away

**Better understanding leads to better solutions — and there are many tools to reach that understanding.** Recapping the toolkit:

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Look for alternate causes** | Don't anchor too fast; stay open to a cause you haven't considered. |
| **Emotions as information** | Treat emotions as smoke detectors signaling something real. |
| **The Inner Why** | Catch the moment an emotion shifts and ask why, in real time. |
| **ABC Analysis** | Map Activating event → Beliefs → Consequences in recurring situations. |
| **Bright Spot Analysis** | Study the times things go *better* to find hidden causes. |

## Q&A highlights

**Is self-help "right-wing coded"?**
Spencer doesn't think the political left is less interested in self-improvement — it's more about which *figures and subcultures* resonate (e.g., Jordan Peterson appeals more to young men on the right). One real difference: progressives more often attribute problems to **systemic** causes, conservatives to **individual** ones. Both can be valid. His view: even when systemic forces are genuinely hurting you, there are often still individual steps that make things easier — so it's rarely hopeless.

**How do you avoid analysis paralysis?**
Separate two modes. There's an **analysis** mode (understanding the problem) and an **action/testing** mode (trying a solution and gathering real-world data). Paralysis comes from never leaving analysis. Form a hypothesis, *stop analyzing*, go test it, then return to analysis with what you learned.

**Tips for building deep, lasting friendships?**
Diagnose *which stage* you struggle with: (1) **meeting** people, (2) **building** a relationship from an acquaintance, or (3) **deepening** it beyond the superficial. Each stage has different solutions — figure out which one is your bottleneck. (The book has a full section on this.)

**Big emotions from small problems — miscalibration or hidden depth?**
Two possibilities. Either your "smoke detector" is **over-sensitive** (from past trauma or biology), in which case techniques can help recalibrate it — or the small thing is a **stand-in for a bigger issue**. Example: a partner not smiling at you triggers intense hurt because, deep down, you fear they don't love you. The small event is just a reminder of the real, larger problem.

**When should I stop DIY and get help?**
Think in three layers based on severity and progress:
1. **Self-help** — fine for less severe challenges; often the most expedient first step.
2. **Coaching** — when you've worked at it a while without progress, or simply want a smart, empathetic person on your team. (Clearer Thinking offers a vetted coaching service.)
3. **Therapy / psychiatry** — for severe issues (persistent depression, suicidality, difficulty functioning). Here, learn what the research says about your specific challenge and seek a specialist — e.g., **exposure** for anxiety, **behavioral activation** for depression.
