SPENCER: There are all these borderline cases where it's sort of debatable whether someone was a victim or what their role was, et cetera. A particularly interesting thing here is even in really clear-cut cases where someone is clearly a victim. Let's say they were walking down the street one night in a safe neighborhood and they got attacked randomly. They're clearly a victim, no question. I think that framing themselves as a victim can actually sometimes be harmful to themselves, even though they're clearly a victim. For example, if they think of it as "there's nothing I can do to prevent this" and if that's what victim means to them, then that could be harmful because even though they didn't deserve it, they didn't cause it. There may be things they could do to stay safer in the future. That just might be wise. If, for example, in their area, there's a series of these attacks going around, they could take proactive action to try to reduce it, even though they're not responsible, even though they're not to blame at all. Sometimes we want there to be this dichotomy: either you're a victim and there's nothing you can do, or you're not a victim. But actually, you can be a victim, and also there's something you can do, and actually there's something you really should do just for your own benefit. Try to protect yourself.
