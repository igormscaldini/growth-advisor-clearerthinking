The short answer

Among readers who are stopped by a paywall, the measured share who then pay is about 0.2% to 0.5% per month at the median publisher, and 1.3% to 1.9% at the top decile. Every multi-publisher dataset that actually defines the metric lands in that band: Lenfest (500+ publishers) median 0.54%, Mather (103 markets) median 0.22%, a one-year clickstream study of a major US newspaper 0.2% "on the spot". The only source claiming 1% to 5% cites no data.

These figures come from news publishers, because no Substack or newsletter source publishes "readers who reached the paywall" as a denominator. Substack's dashboard exposes only per-post views (repeat-counted) and per-post paid sign-ups. The one writer who publishes both shows 0.025% paid per view on her most-viewed post and at least 0.37% on her best-converting one, a 15x spread inside one publication.

How the funnel decomposes

  • Hit the wall, then click "subscribe": 0.2% market average (Poool) to 0.8% (computed from 3.5 million paywall hits across 21 German and Austrian sites). Under 1 in 100 stopped readers starts the checkout.
  • Reach the offer page, then pay: about 10% on desktop and 5% on mobile at 10 US metro dailies. Nine in ten who start, quit.
  • Net, per stop, immediately: roughly 0.01% to 0.08%. The monthly paid-stop rates above (0.2% to 0.5%) are higher because they also count readers who come back and subscribe later or through another door.
  • Per visitor, all traffic: an order of magnitude lower again: 0.05% of unique users per month (Mather), 0.22% for anonymous visitors (Piano). Known or registered users convert about 45x better (9.88%), which matters for a newsletter: the reader hitting a paywall in an email they signed up for is a known user, not an anonymous visitor.

Why the "5 to 10%" number you may have seen is a different metric

Substack's guidance ("we tend to see 5-10% of free subscribers convert to paying subscriptions") is a list-level stock: paid subscribers as a share of the free list, accumulated over the life of a publication. It is not per post and not per paywall hit, and it is undated and unmethoded. Independent data puts the list-level median far lower: 3% from hundreds of self-reported Substacks (with only 20% of publications above 5%), and 0.62% across beehiiv's paid publications. Writers who publish their numbers skew successful; the one small publication in the set that reported after 14 months of paywalling sat at 0.4% and turned paid off.

What moves the per-hit rate

  • How much you give away. In the peer-reviewed German data, showing a deck cut the odds of clicking "subscribe" by 86% and an intro paragraph by 72%. Two creator anecdotes point the other way (moving the wall to just before the payoff raised first-hour sign-ups from 0 to 4 and 2 to 6). Both agree the placement matters more than the platform average.
  • Discounts and trials. A discount raised the odds of completing payment 3.35x. Substack's own figure for its free-month offer: 5% of people who see it take it, and over half of those renew.
  • Who is reading. Email-referred visitors convert at 0.62% vs 0.06% from Facebook (Piano, Q4 2019 medians). Opinion readers subscribe at nearly twice the rate of national-news readers. One writer found 58% of her new paid subscribers were existing free readers.
  • Tightening the wall. More stops beat a higher per-stop rate: at 91 sites that tightened, hits doubled, the per-hit rate fell 20%, and sign-ups rose 46%. At the NYT, tightening cut content demand 9.9% and raised subscriptions 31%. The costs are real: 59% of stopped readers leave immediately, the NYT paywall cut unique visitors 16.8%, and a hard wall on local news cut visits 51%.

How to read this report

Every row links to the page the number was read on and carries the exact quote (expand "Quote from source"). Rows marked "computed from reported counts" are arithmetic on figures the source states; the source itself does not report the rate. Grades rank evidence quality, not relevance: several grade-D creator anecdotes are the only Substack-specific per-post data that exist.
