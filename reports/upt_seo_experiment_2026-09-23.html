Can you buy a Google ranking with real human clicks?

GuidedTrack program 31723 · “UPT - SEO Experiment” · 3,443 runs, Feb 2025 – Sep 2026 · 2026-09-23 10:28am

The experiment paid 2,928 people to Google “personality test”, hunt down the Clearer Thinking result, click it and report their first impression. They did exactly that: each run left roughly one real click in Search Console. The ranking never moved. What the experiment did buy, almost by accident, is the single largest body of cold first impressions of the Ultimate Personality Test that exists.

What the program actually does

It is a four-screen Positly task, not an A/B test. There is no randomisation, no control group and no variant: every participant sees the same instructions. The only recorded outcome is one free-text answer.

  1. Google it. Search the exact phrase personality test, then look through the first, second or third page of results for the Clearer Thinking entry (shown as a screenshot of the SERP snippet).
  2. Try it. Open the test and answer the first three questions only, repeated three times in the copy and capitalised for emphasis.
  3. Report. One paragraph question: “In a few words, what was your first impression of the Personality Test?”
  4. Get paid. A Positly completion link.

The target is programs.clearerthinking.org/personality-test.html, titled “Free Scientific Personality Test: Briggs-Myers, Big 5 & More”. Because participants reach it through a genuine Google search rather than a supplied link, every run leaves a fingerprint in Search Console. That fingerprint is what makes the rest of this analysis possible.

Participation

Two bursts separated by a six-month silence: Feb–Sep 2025 (2,549 runs) and Apr–Aug 2026 (893 runs). Nothing ran between October 2025 and March 2026, which turns out to be the most useful part of the dataset: it is a clean six-month control window.

Question 1: did people really do the task?

Yes. Each run produced about 0.8 real Google clicks. Regressing daily Search Console clicks on the daily run count, controlling for impressions and day of week: +0.82 clicks per run (95% CI 0.57 to 1.06, p = 2×10-10, n = 275 days). The interval comfortably contains 1.0.

This is the compliance check, and it is unusually clean. Participants were never given a link, so the only way a click could appear is if someone genuinely searched, found the result and clicked it. A coefficient near 1.0 means roughly four out of five runs converted into a real search click; the shortfall is the people who gave up, mis-clicked or found the page another way.

An independent and much simpler method agrees almost exactly. Comparing weekday run-days against weekday quiet-days (no regression at all) gives +0.81 clicks per run. Impressions were slightly lower on run-days, so the extra clicks are not an artefact of more traffic.

Question 2: did those clicks move the ranking?

No. The effect is indistinguishable from zero at every lag tested. Across lags of 0 to 28 days, every point estimate is slightly positive (a worse rank) and every 95% interval straddles zero. At the 7-day lag, a 300-run month buys +0.90 positions (95% CI −1.07 to +2.88): the wrong sign, and the interval rules out any gain larger than about one position.

The six-month gap says the same thing

If the clicks were propping up the ranking, position should have decayed once the runs stopped in October 2025. It did not.

Position was essentially flat across all three regimes: 7.92 while running, 8.32 through six months of complete silence, 8.02 when running resumed. The apparent collapse in click-through rate during the gap is not a ranking story either, as the next section explains.

Why it was never going to work

The arithmetic was decisive before any statistics. Over wave 2, the experiment contributed 893 runs against 1.61 million impressions for that query.

The experiment was 9.5% of the clicks but 0.055% of the impressions. Whatever Google measures, it measures it against the whole population of searchers, and a 0.05% nudge is far below the noise floor of a query that swings by hundreds of thousands of impressions month to month. Detecting an effect this small would require orders of magnitude more participants than the budget allows.

An anomaly worth separating out

Between October 2025 and March 2026 impressions for this one query jumped roughly fivefold while clicks stayed flat, dragging measured CTR from 0.76% to 0.14%. That was entirely desktop: desktop impressions went from 256k to 4.12 million over Oct–Nov while desktop CTR collapsed from 1.13% to 0.06%. Mobile was untouched over the same window (613k to 653k impressions, CTR steady at 0.72%).

This is a Google SERP or reporting change, not anything the experiment did, and it has nothing to do with the six-month pause coinciding with it. It matters here for one reason: any before-and-after CTR comparison spanning that window is meaningless. Both active windows sit outside it, so the estimates above are unaffected. It is worth a separate look on its own merits, though: 4.1 million desktop impressions at 0.06% CTR is essentially phantom visibility.

What the experiment actually bought

2,948 unprompted first impressions from people who had just found the test cold in a search result, which is the closest thing available to an organic visitor. Answers were coded into themes with a rule-based coder; on a 50-answer sample I read and coded by hand it agrees 98% of the time.

The good news: the first three questions land

A third of all answers reach for easy, simple or straightforward, and a fifth say interesting or engaging. Several volunteer that they wanted to keep going, which is exactly the reaction the opening of a long test needs to produce.

The problem: one in five says it looks like everything else

18.1% of answers (534 people) describe the test as generic, standard, typical or “like every other personality test”. Only a quarter of those were positive overall. This is the clearest, most actionable finding in the dataset: at the moment of first contact, the thing CT believes differentiates this test (Jungian plus Enneagram plus Big Five in one scientifically-grounded report) is not reaching the person taking it.

The mechanism is visible in the answers: the first three questions are ordinary agree/disagree personality items, indistinguishable from any free quiz. Whatever makes the test different arrives later, after the point where a cold visitor has already decided what kind of site this is.

A small but sharp trust problem

2.2% raise trust concerns unprompted: ads, a suspected paywall, or what happens to their email. The number is small, but these are people who have not yet been asked for anything.

Two flaws in the instrument

It never asks the one question it was designed to answer design

Participants are told to look through the first, second or third page of results, an explicit admission that finding the result is the hard part, and then are never asked whether they found it, where, or how long it took. There is no “which page was it on?”, no “how many results did you scroll past?”. The findability data the design implies it is collecting simply is not collected. Median completion was 2.3 minutes, which tells us the task was not onerous, but nothing about the search itself.

The sample is 92% desktop; the real audience is 63% mobile sampling

Positly panellists work on computers: 92.4% of runs were desktop, 6.7% smartphone. Real search traffic for this query is the mirror image: 63.0% mobile against 35.1% desktop. Every first impression in this dataset is of the desktop experience, which is the minority experience. The mobile first impression, which is what most real visitors get, is unmeasured. The sample is also 100% US, against 56% of real impressions.

What I would do next

1. Stop running it as an SEO experiment high confidence

The ranking hypothesis is answered: no detectable effect, and the scale arithmetic says none was possible. Further runs under this design buy nothing on that question. Roughly 2,600 paid runs went into answering it; that is enough.

2. Re-run it as a first-impression instrument, on mobile high value

The qualitative return is genuinely valuable and cheap. Recruit for smartphone, and add the questions the current version is missing: which result page it was on, whether anything made them hesitate, and what they expected to get. A few hundred mobile runs would be more informative than the 2,549 desktop runs of wave 1.

3. Treat “generic” as the conversion problem it is actionable

One in five cold visitors classifies the test as interchangeable within three questions. That is a first-screen copy and sequencing problem, not a ranking problem. The differentiators need to appear before or during the opening questions, not in the results. This is testable directly on live traffic, with far more statistical power than any panel.

4. Point the SEO effort at the desktop impression anomaly instead investigate

4.1 million desktop impressions at 0.06% CTR over two months is a far larger lever than anything a panel could move, and nobody appears to have looked at what changed. Worth its own diagnosis.

5. Fix the stale URL in the tools index housekeeping

CT_TOOLS.md lists the test as personality.html. That URL still works for a human (it 301-redirects), but the canonical page that ranks and carries the traffic is personality-test.html, and Search Console reports only against the canonical. Querying the listed URL returns essentially zero impressions, so any analysis keyed on it finds nothing and gives no hint that it is looking in the wrong place. This cost me a detour in this analysis.

Method and caveats

  • Sources. GuidedTrack CSV export for program 31723 (all non-test runs, including unfinished ones), and the Search Console sc-domain:clearerthinking.org property, filtered to the exact query and the target page.
  • Windows. Estimates are fitted only on the 275 days inside the two active windows (Jun–Sep 2025 and Apr–Aug 2026), both outside the desktop-impression anomaly.
  • Search Console retention is 16 months, so the first three months of wave 1 (Feb–Apr 2025, 856 runs) have no matching ranking data and are excluded from the estimates though not from the participation counts.
  • Confounding. Runs cluster on weekdays and weekday traffic is heavier, which on its own would manufacture a click effect; day-of-week is therefore a control, and a test asserts that the uncontrolled version does find a spurious effect where the controlled one does not.
  • Not an A/B test. There is no control group inside the program, so every ranking claim rests on time-series comparison and inherits its weaknesses. The null result is the robust part; a true effect smaller than roughly one position per 300-run month would not be detectable here.
  • Text coding is rule-based, not a language model: reproducible and unit-tested, but blunt. Validated at 98% exact agreement against 50 answers hand-coded from a random sample.
  • Code. seo_experiment_analysis.py, with 65 tests in tests/test_seo_experiment_analysis.py covering hand-computed cases and synthetic null/positive datasets.
