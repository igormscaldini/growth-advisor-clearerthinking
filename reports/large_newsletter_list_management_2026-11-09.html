Big newsletters mail readers; Clearer Thinking mails a list

2026-11-09 08:50am · How newsletters above a million subscribers run their lists and sends, set against a read-only audit of CT's beehiiv account (2026-11-06)

Igor's feeling is justified, and the gap is specific. Newsletters above a million subscribers mail only people who recently opened or clicked, and they remove everyone else on a short, automatic clock: Morning Brew removed anyone with no open in 60 days after one last email and a 48-hour wait, and 1440 removed new sign-ups after 14 days or 10 unopened emails (Digiday, 2019-10-15; Campaign Monitor, 2020-10-01).

Clearer Thinking (CT) has no removal rule at all and, since Oct 16, 2026, has gone back to mailing its whole list. As of the audit of 2026-11-06, 325,051 of 666,924 newsletter subscribers (49%) sit outside CT's own definition of engaged, 113k to 146k have never opened a single email, the list is at 96.9% of its 700,000 plan ceiling, and the Oct 29 send to the 252,169 least engaged opened at 2.6% (1.0% at Gmail).

Three more departures: CT cannot see how Gmail, which is 70% to 82% of its recipients, rates its mail; a new subscriber's first email is a coaching pitch that loses 1.40% of everyone who receives it; and the send process let an internal test label go out as a subject line and picks subject-line winners on gaps of 0.03 to 0.5 points.

Not everything is off: authentication passes, complaints at the non-Gmail providers are low, and until Oct 8 CT's engaged-only routine matched big-newsletter practice. The problem is that the good habit was manual, was dropped, and nothing automatic stands behind it.

One caution on the evidence: the public record on 1M+ newsletters is thin and old (the named removal rules date from 2018 to 2020), so the recommendations rest on those rules, the mailbox providers' current rules and CT's own experiments. Nothing has been changed on beehiiv; everything below is a proposal for Igor to approve.

CT against 1M+ practice in ten areas

Terms used in this report, defined once:

  • Apple Mail Privacy Protection (MPP): since September 2021 Apple Mail loads email images in the background, so an email counts as "opened" whether or not a person read it. Open rates and open-based rules from before that date are not comparable with today's.
  • Sunset policy: a standing rule for when to stop mailing someone who has stopped reading.
  • Win-back (or re-engagement): one to three emails asking a lapsed reader whether they still want the newsletter, sent before they are removed.
  • Early-life pruning: removing new sign-ups who open nothing in their first weeks.
  • Double opt-in: a new subscriber receives nothing until they click a confirmation link in a first email.
  • SPF and DKIM: the two checks, published in a domain's public settings (DNS), that prove an email really comes from the domain it claims.
  • DMARC: a DNS record that tells mailbox providers what to do with mail that claims to come from your domain but fails those checks (p=none: nothing, p=quarantine: spam folder, p=reject: refuse it), and where to send reports about such mail.
  • BIMI: a DNS record, usually backed by a paid certificate, that shows a brand logo next to the sender name in some inboxes.
  • Google Postmaster Tools: Google's free dashboard of how Gmail treats a sending domain, including the share of recipients who press "Report spam".
  • Dedicated IP: a sending address used by one sender only, so its reputation is not shared with the email platform's other customers.
  • Warming: raising volume gradually on a new domain or IP so mailbox providers learn to trust it.
  • Split test (A/B test): sending two or more versions to small samples to see which does better before the rest of the list gets the winner.
  • Verified clicks: beehiiv's count of unique clicks after filtering out bots and security scanners.

Ranked recommendations: eleven to adopt, six to leave alone

Excluding someone from a send segment is fully reversible and does not lower the subscriber count. Setting a subscription inactive stops the mail and keeps the record and its history. Bulk delete erases the record and cannot be undone.

10/10 1. Make the engaged segment the only routine audience

  • What to do. From the next send, address every article and every One Helpful Idea to one segment and nothing wider: active, flagged for that newsletter, and either signed up in the last 30 days (after the existing 7-day hold) or opened or clicked in the last 90 days. Hold that for six weeks, the period "Why One Helpful Idea opens fell in 2026" (the OHI report, 2026-11-06) sets for its own engaged-only recommendation; it proposes a different rule for "engaged", discussed under the disagreements below. Then widen in steps of about 10% a week to a ceiling of "opened in 90 days, clicked in 180 days or signed up in 30 days". Never again address a post to "CT Audience - All Subscribers" or to "everyone who has not had it yet". Everyone outside the segment is excluded, not deleted, so the step is fully reversible. Check the recipient count beehiiv shows before each send: the audit found posts addressed to a 666,924-member segment reaching 125,521 to 324,457 people with no visible exclusion. If beehiiv's segment builder cannot filter on clicks within a time window (no CT segment does today), start with opens and add clicks once that is confirmed.
  • In plain terms. Gmail decides where to put CT's mail partly from how many recipients ignore it. Mailing people who never open teaches Gmail that CT's mail is unwanted, and that verdict is then applied to the readers who do want it.
  • Who does this. Morning Brew (4M+) "sends only to engaged users" (Hightouch, undated) and removed anyone with no open in 60 days (Digiday, 2019-10-15, before MPP). HubSpot, owner of The Hustle, excludes unengaged contacts from each send by default and keeps them in the database; whether The Hustle's own sends use that setting is not documented (HubSpot, 2026-09-25). Google: "Consider unsubscribing recipients who don't open or read your messages" (sender guidelines). The repair windows come from deliverability practitioners, not from a 1M+ newsletter: Laura Atkins says to send Gmail only people who opened or clicked within 90 days for at least a month (Word to the Wise, 2021-01-27), Al Iverson says recovery now takes 5 to 7 weeks (Spam Resource, 2024-09-24), Atkins suggests re-adding older readers at about 10% a week (Word to the Wise, 2019-02-26, old), and the 30/90/180 ceiling is consultant Matt McGarry's "base sending segment", which he expects to be 50% to 80% of a list (Newsletter Operator, 2025-05-24).
  • CT today. Engaged-only article sends opened at 26% to 30% in late September. Whole-list sends since Oct 16 open at 17% to 20% (the 17% is the Nov 5 edition after less than a day). The Oct 22 whole-list send cost 3,457 unsubscribes and 80 spam reports. The Oct 29 send to the 252,169 least engaged returned 6,485 opens (2.6%, 1.03% at Gmail), 434 verified clicks, 492 unsubscribes and 49 spam reports. CT's own experiments say the same thing as the big newsletters. In the paired sends of April to May 2026 the non-engaged remainder clicked at 60% of the engaged rate (four April pairs) and cost 1.7x the unsubscribes and 2.9x the spam reports per email (all eight pairs; CT send-strategy report, 2026-09-25). And since July, mailing more people has returned fewer opens: the Aug 10 send to 463,870 got 13,727 fewer unique opens than the Aug 5 send to 234,362 (OHI report).
  • For. Big-newsletter practice, Google's guidance and CT's own causal evidence all point the same way, and the cost is small: the Oct 29 send bought 434 clicks.
  • Against. Recipients per send fall by roughly half, which matters wherever reach is quoted on recipients instead of opens. Open-based rules misjudge some real readers: Proton addresses show 0.2% to 7% opens on every send because they block images, so clicks must count too. The size of a 90-day segment is unknown until it is built (no recency segment exists today except the six-month rule).
  • Score: 10/10.

9/10 2. See what Gmail sees

  • What to do. Verify both ohi.clearerthinking.net and the root clearerthinking.net in Google Postmaster Tools (one DNS record each) and read the user-reported spam rate and the compliance status. The root matters because Google's dashboard covers only the exact domain used for authentication (Google, paraphrased) and CT's DKIM signature sits on the root. Then ask beehiiv's Enterprise team for a deliverability review and three answers: why the domain still shows warming_up, whether CT is on a shared pool or a dedicated IP, and what the 9k to 18k "suppressions" per large send consist of. After each send, log Gmail against Microsoft plus Yahoo opens and clicks from beehiiv's provider breakdown (the OHI report's method). Act on written thresholds: Gmail spam rate 0.10% (investigate) and 0.30% (ceiling), bounce rate about 2%, unsubscribe rate 0.3%, opens 10 or more points below normal.
  • In plain terms. beehiiv's spam-report counts leave Gmail out, so today nobody at CT knows how many Gmail readers press "Report spam". Postmaster Tools is the only place that number exists.
  • Who does this. 1440 (3.6M subscribers, 99% of revenue from email) uses Validity Everest for inbox placement per provider and list validation, and its team gets an email every morning with oversight of its sending backend (Validity, 2024-08; Campaign Monitor, 2020-10-01). The thresholds are Google's (sender guidelines) and beehiiv's (diagnostic guide, 2026-10-09). beehiiv lists "direct access to deliverability specialists" and quarterly deliverability reviews for Enterprise accounts (beehiiv Enterprise). Deliverability consultant Al Iverson recommends Postmaster Tools for "everyone with a domain who sends email to Gmail subscribers" and calls paid inbox-placement tests (test mailboxes that show whether mail lands in the inbox, Promotions or spam) "expensive, and occasionally imperfect" (Spam Resource, 2025-06-23), which fits the 6/10 the OHI report gives such a test.
  • CT today. Gmail is 70% to 82% of recipients. Spam reports in beehiiv run 0.01 to 0.2 per 1,000 delivered and exclude Gmail. Postmaster Tools registration, IP setup and the meaning of warming_up are all unknown.
  • For. Free, a matter of minutes, and it confirms or kills the diagnosis every other recommendation depends on. The OHI report scores it 9/10 and nothing in the big-newsletter evidence argues otherwise.
  • Against. It shows how bad things are, not how to fix them, and holds no history if the domain was never verified. Its reputation charts may be gone: a second-hand account says they were retired with version 1 on 2025-09-30 (EmailKarma, 2025-09-25) while Google's help page still describes them (Google). The spam-rate view is the dependable part.
  • Score: 9/10.

9/10 3. Welcome first, pitch later

  • What to do. In the "[Drip Campaign] New Subscribers" automation, replace "Big news" as the first email. Minute 0: a welcome of 100 to 300 words that says why they are getting it (they used a CT tool), what CT sends and when (two emails a week), gives one link back to something useful, asks for a one-word reply or a move to the Primary tab, and shows a plain "not for me, unsubscribe" line. Day 2: one best article, chosen by the last_tool_tried field (the personalized copy already exists in the repo). Day 5: one sample One Helpful Idea. Day 7: newsletters begin, as today. Coaching offer no earlier than day 21, and only to people who have opened or clicked since signing up. Five emails in three weeks. Reversible: it is an automation edit. The day-by-day layout and the day-21 rule for the coaching offer are this report's design, built from the published ranges below, not a schedule any big newsletter has published.
  • Who does this. Morning Brew's and The Hustle's welcome emails do the same six things: thanks, intro, send time, add to contacts, move to Primary, reply (The Newsletter Newsletter, 2023-08-25, second-hand observation). The add-to-contacts ask was part of Morning Brew's 2018 deliverability fix (Campaign Monitor, 2018-05-01, old). The sequence rules are consultant guidance from Matt McGarry: 3 to 7 emails, "99% free value and 1% sales" (Newsletter Operator, 2024-04-13), with a first email of 100 to 300 words (Newsletter Operator, 2026-09-11). For people who never saw the newsletter's own sign-up page, SparkLoop tells its customers to use a separate, context-heavy welcome with an obvious unsubscribe button (SparkLoop, 2026-06-03). Not documented: any 1M+ newsletter that grows through quizzes or tools and explains its onboarding.
  • CT today. The automation sends nothing at sign-up. At 24 hours it sends the coaching offer: 110,684 delivered, 1,545 unsubscribes (1.40%, 4.6 to 6 times the later drip emails), 28 spam reports, and 68% of its clicks are on the manage-preferences link. Its coaching links drew 283 clicks (183 unique). Two of the three emails a new subscriber gets in the first 30 days are about coaching. 119,302 people have been enrolled and 45,673 are in it now.
  • For. It touches every new subscriber (about 500 a day), the measured cost of the current first email is 1,545 lost subscribers for 183 unique coaching clickers, and the replacement copy is written.
  • Against. No 1M+ newsletter has published a controlled result showing that onboarding raises long-run engagement. The coaching offer loses its largest audience by moving later and to fewer people.
  • Score: 9/10.

8/10 4. Run a sunset policy: exclude now, win back in batches, do not delete

  • What to do. Step 1 is recommendation 1: the inactive are already excluded. Step 2: rebuild the feeding segment with a status filter (active, on the list 90 days or more, no open or click in 90 days, which is beehiiv's default trigger). Split it in two. People who have never opened (113k to 146k) get one last-chance email with a single "keep me subscribed" button and are set inactive after 7 days without a click. People who used to open and stopped (roughly 180k to 210k: 325,051 minus the never-opened range, approximate because the segments were calculated on different dates) get two emails 4 to 7 days apart, then the same. Enrol them in weekly batches, not at once, and start only after recommendation 2 has shown where Gmail stands, because these are low-open sends from the damaged domain by design. Export the segment before any status change. Step 3: leave a permanent automation on beehiiv's unengaged trigger so the policy runs without anyone remembering it. Exclusion is reversible; inactive status keeps the record; nothing here deletes anyone. The two-track split (one email for never-openers, two for lapsed readers) is this report's design from the win-back lengths cited below, not a published rule.
  • Who does this. Morning Brew: 60 days, one email, 48 hours; Tyler Denk says he "churned nearly 100,000 un-engaged subscribers" and opens went from about 20% to about 45%, alongside a new platform, sending domain and dedicated IP, so the separate effect is unknown (Digiday, 2019-10-15; Denk, about 2018). The Rundown AI cleans weekly (sales page). beehiiv's own guidance is removal after 30 to 60 days of inactivity (beehiiv webinar, 2025-01-28) and a default trigger at 90 days (beehiiv support, 2026-06-22). Win-back length: one email (Morning Brew), two (McGarry, 2023-11-18), three on days 0, 4 and 7 (beehiiv blog). Dan Oshinsky, former newsletter director at BuzzFeed and The New Yorker, gives 90 days as the baseline and 4 to 6 months for weekly senders, with 2% to 5% won back on a first campaign (Inbox Collective, 2023-04-05).
  • CT today. No win-back is live. The sunset flow ran from Jun 18 to Jun 26, 2026: its two emails went to 21,646 and 19,296 people and opened at 1.83% and 1.19%, and it was switched off with 16,735 people still in the final wait. The 126,284 people with a lifetime open rate of zero and more than 13 weeks on the list (117,248 of them still subscribed) had been sent 3,323,188 emails by Oct 7. The list is at 678,349 of 700,000, about three weeks of headroom at the current net growth of 28k per 4 weeks.
  • For. It is the most consistently documented practice at this scale, Google and Yahoo both recommend it, and it frees 113k or more places under the plan ceiling weeks before CT hits it (if the ceiling counts active subscribers, which the audit could not confirm).
  • Against. Win-back yields are small: The Wall Street Journal recovered fewer than 1% on lists that had never been cleaned (Digiday, 2019-10-15). Part of any open-rate rise is arithmetic, not new readers. The headline list size could fall by up to half. All named-operator rules predate MPP, and an open-based rule now keeps every Apple Mail address forever.
  • Score: 8/10.

8/10 5. Prune new sign-ups who never open (early-life pruning)

  • What to do. Build a separate automation, because beehiiv's stock unengaged trigger skips recent sign-ups (beehiiv support, 2026-06-22). Trigger: sign-up. Wait 35 days, which at CT's cadence is about 10 emails (the drip plus two newsletters a week from day 7). If the subscriber has no open and no click, send one "do you still want this?" email with a stay button, wait 7 days, and set the subscription inactive if there is no click. The record stays; nothing is deleted.
  • In plain terms. Someone who opens none of their first ten emails almost never starts later. Removing them at five weeks stops the pile of never-openers from growing back after recommendation 4 clears it.
  • Who does this. 1440: "14 days (10 messages)" before automatic removal (Campaign Monitor, 2020-10-01). Morning Brew: three weeks to open a first email, then the 48-hour process; Axios: a check-in at one week and removal of non-responders at two, with its 43% open rate held before and after (Digiday, 2019-10-15). All three are dailies and all three rules predate MPP; none has been restated since. The slower consultant rule is McGarry's "remove non-openers after 30-60 days" (Newsletter Operator, 2023-02-18). The 35-day figure is this report's translation of 1440's ten-message count to CT's cadence, not a published rule.
  • CT today. About 500 sign-ups a day (median 465; 31,152 on Oct 21 to 23), 97% through the API from tools, single opt-in. Nothing removes a new subscriber who never opens. In the last 4 weeks beehiiv reports 52,707 new subscribers but only 39,074 still active by source, which suggests about one in four has already left (the audit's inference).
  • For. It is the best-documented practice in the public record, and CT's intake (tool users who gave an email to get a result) is exactly the kind of cohort these rules were written for.
  • Against. Apple Mail's automatic opens pass the test without a human, so some dead addresses survive. Readers who block images look dead and get one extra email. It adds an automation that someone must own.
  • Score: 8/10.

7/10 6. Fix the schedule and lock the send

  • What to do. Publish and hold fixed slots: the article on Thursday at one set time, One Helpful Idea on one set weekday and time. Put promotional emails on one fixed day that carries no edition. Lock content the day before. Adopt a one-page pre-send checklist: the subject line is not the post title and carries no bracketed label; the audience segment and the recipient count are what was intended; split-test variants differ; sponsor box and links are this week's; a test email was read on a phone; test posts are deleted or hidden from the public feed. For minor errors, do not send a correction to the whole list.
  • Who does this. James Clear (3M+) sends "once a week on Thursdays" (sales page); Mark Manson (1M) every Monday (sales page); Farnam Street (950,000+, just under the 1M line) on Sunday (sales page); Tim Ferriss every Friday (sign-up page). The Rundown AI keeps its dedicated sponsor email to Sundays so it never displaces an edition (sales page). Morning Brew queued each issue at 8 to 9pm for the next morning (Thrive Global, 2019-06-18). The correction rule is Dan Oshinsky's (Inbox Collective, 2022-09-21). Thin evidence: no 1M+ newsletter has published a checklist, a promo frequency cap or a send-time test.
  • CT today. Thursday evening is the usual article slot, but 4 of the 7 editions since Sep 18 went out at other times (Friday evening, Tuesday evening, Thursday afternoon, Friday morning). One Helpful Idea drifted from Monday 7pm to Tuesday 11pm ET, and none has gone out since Oct 20. The Oct 8 test label reached the rest of 164,264 recipients as a subject line; the Nov 5 variant titles are listed on the public beehiiv site; 15 test posts with 4 to 6 recipients went through the live publication in 60 days.
  • For. It costs nothing, and the errors it prevents were seen by six-figure audiences.
  • Against. There is no published evidence that a consistent slot raises engagement, and a checklist adds friction for a two-person team.
  • Score: 7/10.

7/10 7. Add a DMARC reporting address and clean up the DNS

  • What to do. Add a rua= reporting address to the DMARC records of clearerthinking.net and clearerthinking.org, pointing at a DMARC report reader. Read two to four weeks of reports to see every system that sends as CT. Only then remove Mailchimp (servers.mcsv.net) and Mailgun from the SPF record and retire the Mailchimp DKIM keys, if nothing legitimate still uses them. Confirm the reply-to address, because ohi.clearerthinking.net has no mail server of its own to receive replies. Skip BIMI for now.
  • In plain terms. Today nobody at CT is told when some other system (an old Mailchimp account, a forgotten tool or a spammer) sends email claiming to be clearerthinking.net. A reporting address switches those notifications on, and the cleanup closes doors that have stood open since the move to beehiiv.
  • Who does this. Of 20 large senders checked in DNS on 2026-11-06, 13 publish p=reject, 5 p=quarantine and 2 p=none (Milk Road's root and James Clear), and nearly all send reports to a monitoring vendor: Morning Brew to dmarcian and Cloudflare, The Hustle to Valimail, Axios to EasyDMARC, 1440 to Validity Everest. Yahoo calls a working rua tag "strongly recommended" (Yahoo Sender Hub). BIMI is not standard at this tier: only four of the domains checked have a certificate-backed record (Washington Post, The Rundown AI, Industry Dive's newsletter domain, HubSpot), and Morning Brew, theSkimm, Axios and The New York Times publish none.
  • CT today. clearerthinking.net publishes v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; with no reporting address; clearerthinking.org is p=none, also without one. SPF still authorizes Mailchimp and Mailgun a year after the move, and Mailchimp DKIM keys are still published. The sending subdomain has no mail-server (MX), SPF, DKIM or DMARC record of its own. SPF and DKIM pass and one-click unsubscribe (an unsubscribe that works from the mail app's own button) is present on delivered copies (OHI report).
  • For. One line of DNS tests a live hypothesis: Laura Atkins's Gmail repair guide says that when engaged-only sending does not help, look for mail you do not know about that uses your domain (Word to the Wise, 2021-01-27).
  • Against. CT already meets every binding rule (the providers require only p=none), so this is unlikely to be the cause of the Gmail drop. Reports need a reader, and removing an SPF entry that something still uses would break that mail.
  • Score: 7/10.

6/10 8. Read every source as a cohort, and remove the known junk

  • What to do. Create one saved segment per source: each major tool (from last_tool_tried), each import batch, each ad campaign (from the UTM fields, the campaign tags carried by sign-up links). Once such a group (a cohort) has 200 or more subscribers and 30 or more days on the list, record its open, click and unsubscribe rates next to the list average, monthly. A source that lags badly gets a gate (recommendation 13, for that source only) or a shorter early-life window. Separately, set inactive the 772 addresses on disposable domains that beehiiv flagged, and exclude the eight look-alike domains with zero opens. Ask the tools' developers what validation runs before the beehiiv API call.
  • Who does this. 1440 compares sources on open rate and cost: newsletter-sourced subscribers open at about 2x paid-social ones (Newsletter Circle, 2024-02-23). Milk Road judged paid channels on engagement and unsubscribe rates (beehiiv case study, 2025-04-07). Axios's core acquisition metric was cost per engaged email acquired (Nieman Lab, 2019-06-10). The 200-subscriber and 30-day thresholds are McGarry's (Newsletter Operator, 2023-04-08). Not documented: any 1M+ operator's cut-off for dropping a source.
  • CT today. 554,993 active subscribers (82%) carry the import channel and 121,722 (18%) came through the API. "Import" is not the same as "cold": it also holds the original list migrated to beehiiv, which is one more reason to split it into batches. No send audience distinguishes any of them. The 772 flagged addresses have received 9,121 emails since February and produced 12 opens. Eight look-alike domains (about 900 to 1,600 addresses per send) show exactly zero opens on all four whole-list sends from Oct 22 to Nov 6.
  • For. It tells CT which tools produce readers and which produce dead addresses, the input for every acquisition decision, and the junk removal is small and unambiguous.
  • Against. CT must set its own bar, since nobody publishes one. It is recurring analysis work, and its effect is smaller than recommendations 1 to 5.
  • Score: 6/10.

6/10 9. Report engaged readers, not list size

  • What to do. Build one weekly scorecard from the beehiiv API and manage to it: unique opens and verified clicks per edition, Gmail's open rate as a share of the other providers', unsubscribes per 1,000, engaged subscribers against active subscribers, new sign-ups and the share of last month's sign-ups who have opened anything. Quote engaged readers and opens to sponsors and on the sign-up page. Fix or delete segments whose names and rules disagree.
  • Who does this. Morning Brew publicised "1.6 million active subscribers" against over 3 million subscribers across its lifetime and called unique opens "the only metric that really matters" (Digiday, 2019-10-15; Talking Biz News, 2019-10-17). Farnam Street reports "effective readers", subscribers times open rate (annual letter, 2019-12-20). 1440 sells ads on opens (Campaign Monitor, 2020-10-01). All of this is 2019 to 2020; no 1M+ newsletter has published its dashboard or review routine.
  • CT today. 678,349 active against 341,873 engaged by CT's own rule. The publication-level open rate is 18.3% over the last 4 weeks against 29.3% over 12 months. The public description says "over 300,000 subscribers". "Very Engaged Audience" (open rate 90% or more) is 51% people who have unsubscribed.
  • For. The number a team manages to decides its behaviour, and a list-size number is what makes a whole-list send look like reach.
  • Against. It is reporting and changes nothing by itself. Opens are inflated by MPP, so the scorecard needs clicks beside them.
  • Score: 6/10.

5/10 10. Test less, and only when the test can be read

  • What to do. Run a subject-line test only with two different lines, a test sample of at least 20% of the audience (about 34k per line on an engaged audience of roughly 340k; this report's arithmetic, not a published rule), and the longest window beehiiv offers (240 minutes). Treat the result as "no difference" unless beehiiv reports a statistically significant winner. Stop splitting an edition into two posts sent hours apart: Gmail treated the two halves so differently that neither rating-box test can be read. Hold all testing until the first send of the day stops opening at 5% to 9% at Gmail.
  • Who does this. Morning Brew's 2020 protocol used cells of roughly 75,000 people (AWeber via mirror, 2021-04-20, second-hand, before MPP). beehiiv's mechanics: default 10% sample and 60 minutes, selectable up to 240, the test cannot be ended early, and without a significant winner beehiiv sends the line with the most opens (beehiiv support, 2026-10-01). Not documented: any 1M+ newsletter's current protocol, or one that picks winners on clicks.
  • CT today. 16 of 23 posts had a split test and 4 tested a line against itself. Winners were sent on 3.15% against 3.45% against 3.66% (about 20.8k per arm) and 8.94% against 8.67% against 8.91% (about 10.9k per arm) after one hour. In the two manual splits the half sent first opened at 9.09% and 6.48% at Gmail and the half sent 71 minutes and 2 hours 16 minutes later at 22.73% and 24.84%.
  • For. A test that declares winners from noise is worse than no test, because it produces false lessons.
  • Against. A four-hour window delays the main send by four hours. The only named big-newsletter protocol is six years old.
  • Score: 5/10.

5/10 11. Give readers real preferences, including a way out of promos

  • What to do. Replace the two custom-field flags with beehiiv's newsletter lists: the articles, One Helpful Idea, and "offers and events" (coaching, workshops, launches). Link the preferences page from every promo. Do it after recommendations 1 to 5, since every send audience is built on the flags today.
  • Who does this. James Clear lets readers leave 3-2-1 only or all email (unsubscribe page, search snippet). The Marginalian's Sunday and Wednesday emails are separate subscriptions (newsletter page; its list size was not established in this research). Farnam Street offered a 30-day break in 2019 (annual letter). Thin and partly old; no preference-centre design from a 1M+ publisher was found.
  • CT today. No preference lists, no promo opt-out, pauses off, and whether readers can change the two flags themselves is unknown. CT's worst unsubscribe rates are all on coaching emails: 14.4 per 1,000 on the Oct 12 promo to the 100%-open tier, 14.0 per 1,000 on "Big news", against 1.3 to 5.6 on newsletter sends.
  • For. A reader who dislikes coaching offers can currently only leave altogether.
  • Against. Thin evidence, and a migration error could mail or silence the wrong people. A generic source (not a 1M+ operator) found 98% of people offered a pause did not use it (Parcel, search snippet).
  • Score: 5/10.

4/10 12. Get a dedicated IP

  • What to do. Do not request one now. Ask beehiiv what CT is on (recommendation 2) and take one only if beehiiv's deliverability team advises it after the review.
  • In plain terms. A dedicated IP protects a sender from its neighbours' bad behaviour. CT's evidence says the problem is its own domain's standing, which travels with the domain to any IP.
  • Who does this. The Hustle (800,000+ then) moved to dedicated IPs and reported unique opens up 17.6% (Twilio SendGrid, undated); Morning Brew did it in 2018 (Campaign Monitor, 2018-05-01); 1440 certifies its own IPs (Validity, 2024-08). beehiiv requires one only above 700,000 emails a day per publication (beehiiv support, 2026-03-05).
  • CT today. IP setup unknown. Engaged-only sends are 330k to 375k emails. The two whole-list halves on the evening of Nov 5 totalled 648,826, close to beehiiv's threshold, which is one more reason to stop them.
  • For. It is what the large ad-funded dailies did, and it removes dependence on a shared pool.
  • Against. CT's Aug 13 split test sent the same email from the same platform and Gmail opened 31% from one domain and 5% from the other (CT send-strategy report, 2026-09-25): a domain-level problem. A new IP also needs weeks of warming.
  • Score: 4/10.

3/10 13. Turn on double opt-in for every sign-up

  • What to do. Do not turn it on publication-wide. Keep it as a targeted gate: beehiiv can require confirmation per form or per API call (beehiiv support, 2026-10-09), so apply it only to a source that fails the cohort read in recommendation 8.
  • Who does this. Kit makes it the default (Kit, 2026-06-25) and Mark Manson's newsletter on Kit uses it (Growth Currency, 2025-01-16, second-hand). Morning Brew required it for referral sign-ups from 2019 (Digiday, 2019-10-14). Against it: McGarry, "Don't use double opt-in (20-50% won't confirm)" (Newsletter Operator, 2023-04-08). In between: Oshinsky, who reports 80% to 90% confirming at the best organizations and about 50% at average ones, and recommends it for giveaway entrants (Inbox Collective, 2023-06-14).
  • CT today. Off. 97% of sign-ups come through the API from tools.
  • For. A tool user who typed an email to get a result resembles Oshinsky's giveaway entrant more than a newsletter sign-up, and beehiiv's import terms ask for people who "knowingly consented and opted in to your newsletter" (beehiiv support, 2026-10-01).
  • Against. It would cost CT somewhere between a tenth and half of its sign-ups by those estimates, early-life pruning does the same filtering without that loss, and it does not stop bots (Oshinsky, same source).
  • Score: 3/10.

3/10 14. Resend routinely to non-openers

  • What to do. Stop. Allow a resend only for a launch-level announcement, only to non-openers inside the engaged segment, with a new subject line, and not during the six repair weeks.
  • Who does this. No 1M+ newsletter was found documenting resends. Tyler Denk (beehiiv CEO, formerly Morning Brew) calls them "truthfully a terrible idea that should be avoided" and says none of the largest newsletters he has worked with considered them (beehiiv blog, updated 2026-07-12). Dan Oshinsky allows them "sparingly, and only for high-value emails" (Inbox Collective, 2022-12-01).
  • CT today. CT's own experiment points the other way and deserves a fair hearing. The Sep 14 re-launch of the Who Attracts You email went to 232,000 people, opened at 5.8% and added 711 verified clicks, about 22% on top of the 3,191 from the two Sep 10 sends, for 129 unsubscribes (0.56 per 1,000 in the Nov 6 audit; 0.4 when the Sep 25 report counted them) and 1 spam report. The Oct 19 resend of the Supplement Paradox to 47,997 non-openers with a lifetime open rate of 50% or more did worse: 80 verified clicks for 105 unsubscribes.
  • For. The Sep 14 gain was real and its visible cost was tiny.
  • Against. The visible cost is the wrong measure. Unsubscribes and non-Gmail spam reports are not how Gmail penalizes a sender; ignored mail is, and the OHI report names this same re-launch among the sends that "teach Gmail that mail from this domain is mostly ignored" (it was one of three under-15% sends the domain carried in September, the month after its Gmail results broke). This report sides with Denk for routine editions and with Oshinsky's exception for a tool launch once Gmail has recovered. That agrees with the OHI report (keep resends off the domain, 8/10) and overrules the Sep 25 report's "pattern to keep".
  • Score: 3/10.

3/10 15. Build a referral program with rewards

  • What to do. Do not build one now. Switch the empty program off or leave it idle. If it is ever built, count a referral only after the referred address opens or clicks.
  • Who does this. Morning Brew: referrals were 35% of list growth in Oct 2019 at about $0.25 per sign-up in merchandise, against $2 to $5 on paid channels (Digiday, 2019-10-14). The Hustle: referred subscribers opened at about twice the average (Digiday, 2020-07-10). All figures are from 2015 to 2021. Morning Brew had to add double opt-in "to ensure only legitimate referrals get counted" (Referral Rock, updated 2023-08-01, second-hand).
  • CT today. The program is enabled with no milestones and no rewards. The referral channel accounts for 3 active subscribers, all time.
  • For. Where measured, referred readers were the best cohort.
  • Against. CT's constraint is list quality and a plan ceiling 21,651 subscribers away, not sign-up volume. Rewards attract fake sign-ups, and it is a build project for a two-person team.
  • Score: 3/10.

2/10 16. Bulk delete the inactive half now

  • What to do. Do not. Exclusion and inactive status deliver the whole deliverability benefit. Consider deletion only after the win-back has run, with an export saved, and only if beehiiv confirms that inactive records count against the 700,000 ceiling.
  • Who does this. beehiiv's support article suggests bulk deleting subscribers its automation marks inactive, and warns that deletion is permanent (beehiiv support, 2026-06-22). Oshinsky prefers removal because platforms often charge per contact (Inbox Collective, 2023-04-05). McGarry stops short of deletion: "I recommend unsubscribing them and excluding them from future newsletter sends" (Newsletter Operator, 2023-11-18).
  • CT today. 325,051 outside the engaged rule; 113k to 146k never-openers. The open data that would drive the delete is unreliable in both directions: Proton readers show 0.2% to 7% opens, and many school domains show 98% to 100% because security scanners open the mail.
  • For. It is the cleanest end state if the platform bills on every record.
  • Against. It is the one irreversible step in this report, it erases the tool and purchase history stored on each record, and a generic case (not a 1M+ operator) saw paid sign-ups soften after a 30,000-contact delete (Leaky Paywall, 2026-05-12).
  • Score: 2/10.

2/10 17. Move to a fresh sending domain

  • What to do. Do not. Keep ohi.clearerthinking.net and repair it with recommendations 1, 2 and 4.
  • Who does this. Morning Brew's 2018 fix included "moving to a new sending domain", together with about 100,000 removals, a dedicated IP and a platform change, so its separate effect is unknown (Campaign Monitor, 2018-05-01). Practitioners argue against it: "Changing domains is (almost) never the solutions to domain reputation problems" (Laura Atkins, 2019-02-25, old); filters "link a subdomain to the main organizational domain" (Al Iverson, 2026-03-21). On beehiiv a new domain goes through 6 to 8 weeks of automatic warming (beehiiv support, 2026-10-01).
  • CT today. CT has already run this experiment. On Aug 13 the same email to the same audience opened at 31% at Gmail from the newer ohi.clearerthinking.net and 5% from info@clearerthinking.net (44,427 of 143,146 delivered against 7,409 of 142,464; CT send-strategy report, 2026-09-25). The lift was real. It lasted about seven weeks: the main newsletter moved onto the newer domain on Jul 1, and that domain's Gmail results broke between Aug 5 and Aug 18 (OHI report).
  • For. The short-term gain is large and proven on CT's own list.
  • Against. The standing follows the sending habits, not the name. A third domain would buy a few weeks and spend them the same way unless recommendation 1 is in place, and if it is in place the move is unnecessary. The OHI report scores this 2/10 and the outside evidence agrees.
  • Score: 2/10.

What the evidence says, by theme

The sunset rules on record are fast, automatic and mostly from 2018 to 2020

The exact, operator-stated policies are few. Morning Brew moved from a CEO email after six weeks without an open (2018) to an automatic rule (2019): 60 days plus 48 hours for established readers and three weeks for new ones (Digiday, 2018-11-05; Digiday, 2019-10-15). 1440 held "a consistent 55%+ average open rate" with its 14-day rule (Campaign Monitor, 2020-10-01). All of these are dailies, and their clocks do not transfer to CT by the calendar: ten unopened messages is two weeks of a daily and five weeks of CT's two-a-week cadence. HubSpot's send-count rule (unengaged after 11 sends for someone who never engaged, 16 for someone who once did) is the cleanest way to express a window that adapts to cadence (HubSpot, 2026-09-25). The reported results are large and confounded: Morning Brew's rise from about 20% to about 45% opens came with four other changes, and no 1M+ operator has published a result that isolates pruning.

Since Apple's change, "engaged" means a recent click as well as an open

After MPP, Dan Oshinsky's clients saw open rates rise 15% to 20% on average, and 1440's chief operating officer reported unattributable clicks rising from about 5% to about 9% (Digiday, 2022-06-16). The consultants who serve large lists now treat "opens everything, never clicks" as inactive: Oshinsky calls such a reader "an inactive user disguised as an active one" (Inbox Collective, 2023-04-05), and McGarry writes "Do not base your list cleaning and re-engagement on opens alone" (Newsletter Operator, 2023-10-07). CT's data shows the same weakness from its own side: its "100% open rate" tier produced the highest unsubscribe rate of any send (14.4 per 1,000 on Oct 12), because a perfect lifetime rate mostly marks people who received one or two emails, plus scanner-opened addresses. No CT segment uses clicks.

The binding rules are easy to pass; the Gmail repair playbook is what matters

Gmail requires bulk senders (more than 5,000 messages a day) to pass SPF and DKIM, publish DMARC at any level, offer one-click unsubscribe and keep the spam rate under 0.10% and never at 0.30%; above 0.3% a sender is "ineligible for mitigation" until seven consecutive days below it, and enforcement has been "ramping up" since November 2025 (Google; Google FAQ). Yahoo sets 0.3% and Microsoft has applied equivalent authentication rules since 2025-05-05 (Yahoo; Microsoft, 2025-04-02). CT passes the authentication parts. The repair sequence practitioners describe is: confirm where mail lands, send Gmail only recent openers and clickers at normal cadence for at least a month, expect 5 to 7 weeks, then re-add older readers in batches of about 10% a week (Atkins, 2021-01-27; Iverson, 2024-09-24; Atkins, 2019-02-26, old). One caveat for reading CT's trend: beehiiv says "Gmail open rates began declining in early 2026 in ways that don't align with any sender-side changes" (beehiiv diagnostic guide, 2026-10-09). That could account for part of the slow January-to-August drift in the OHI report; it cannot account for the mid-August break, which the OHI report measured against other providers inside the same sends.

Onboarding is a deliverability tool first, and sources are judged on engagement

The welcome emails observed at Morning Brew and The Hustle spend their space on getting a reply, a contact-list add and a move to Gmail's Primary tab; neither surveys the reader, and no sales pitch is among the six elements observed. There is no controlled evidence that these asks move inbox placement, and one deliverability vendor calls chasing the Primary tab wasted effort (Inbox Monster, 2025-09-29), so their value is unproven, cheap and widely assumed. On acquisition, the unit the large operators optimize is cost per engaged subscriber or per open, not cost per sign-up: 1440 reports about $0.05 of revenue per open and a payback of about 4 months on a $3 subscriber (Letterhead, 2026-01-28, second-hand). The one measured import in the record is small and not from a 1M+ operator: an acquired list brought in without a permission step opened at 39.3% against 60% for the buyer's own list and lost about 10.8% in six weeks (Newsletter Operator, 2023-12-30). CT's August 2026 import lost 6.9% of its 173,257 records in its first month.

Send operations: fixed slots, few ads, almost nothing published on QA

The weekly essay newsletters closest to CT in format hold one fixed day and one sponsor: Farnam Street runs "One sponsor per newsletter" (sales page), and TLDR, at the other end, caps at three advertisers and writes the ad copy in-house (sales page). Self-reported open rates on current sales pages cluster at 45% to 55% (James Clear 47% to 49%, Mark Manson 55%, The Rundown AI 54.8% with a 2.5% click rate), figures that include Apple's automatic opens and come from pruned lists, so they are not a target for an unpruned list. The New York Times reported that the "vast majority" of opens arrive within three hours of the send (Digiday, 2021-02-10); no 1M+ newsletter has published a send-time test.

Where the big operators disagree, and what nobody has published

Remove or keep and suppress. The ad-funded dailies whose product is opens (Morning Brew, 1440, Axios) remove fast. Publishers whose free list feeds something else lean toward keeping: SmartBrief's Joe Webster said "If they've shown value historically, trying to keep them in your audience makes a lot of sense" (Digiday, 2019-10-15), and Substack prunes only publications it flags and never paying or manually signed-up readers (Substack help, wording from a search snippet). CT has both sponsor revenue and a paid membership, so it sits between the camps. That is why this report recommends exclusion and inactive status and scores deletion 2/10.

Whether win-back is worth it. The Wall Street Journal recovered under 1% and judged it not worth the effort (Digiday, 2019-10-15); Oshinsky calls it nearly free and reports 5% to 15% when repeated (Inbox Collective, 2023-04-05); McGarry says the odds are low after 30 days and to put the effort into the welcome flow instead (Newsletter Operator, 2023-11-18). The results differ mainly by how stale the list was. CT's never-openers are the stale case, which is why they get one email, not three.

How much to trust opens. McGarry and Oshinsky distrust them; Tyler Denk calls them "less reliable, but still mostly directionally correct" (Splice Media, undated); Al Iverson calls them "good enough" for suppression decisions (Spam Resource, 2024-09-24). Advertisers still buy on opens: publishers told Digiday in 2023 that their advertisers rely on open rates and subscriber cohorts, not click rates (Digiday, 2023-07-28).

Double opt-in, new domains, dedicated IPs, resends. Each has a named big-newsletter user and a named opponent; the positions are set out under recommendations 12 to 17.

Where this evidence departs from CT's own reports. Three points, stated plainly. First, the OHI report suggests that rare blasts could go "from a different subdomain" so the newsletters are not graded with them. No 1M+ newsletter was found doing this, Google counts a subdomain's mail toward the parent domain, and Iverson says filters link the two, so the supported half of that recommendation is "stop them", not "move them". Second, the OHI report proposes the lifetime-open-rate segment (30% or more, about 161k) as the strict audience. The outside evidence defines engagement by recency (opened or clicked in the last 60 to 90 days), which is stricter on people who read in 2025 and stopped and kinder to recent readers; the direction is the same, the rule should be recency. Third, the Sep 25 send-strategy report called a resend to non-openers "the pattern to keep"; the big-newsletter evidence and the OHI report both say otherwise.

What the public record does not cover. No named 1M+ newsletter has restated its sunset or early-life rule since MPP. The weekly essay newsletters most like CT (James Clear, Mark Manson, Farnam Street, Sahil Bloom, Tim Ferriss, Lenny's Newsletter) have published nothing on list hygiene. Nobody at this scale documents a quiz or tool sign-up funnel, a pre-send checklist, a promo frequency cap, a send-time test, a preference-centre design, a threshold for cutting an acquisition source, or a Gmail incident and recovery after 2018. Several consultant sources (Matt McGarry, Dan Oshinsky) advise large newsletters but are not themselves 1M+ operators, and McGarry's client list was not verified.

What to check about CT before changing anything

These are the audit's open questions, in the order they affect the recommendations.

  1. Google Postmaster Tools. Whether either domain is registered, and what the Gmail spam rate is. Everything about the size and urgency of the Gmail problem depends on it.
  2. Why whole-list sending resumed on Oct 16. Nothing readable records the decision. If it was deliberate, whoever made it should see the Oct 29 result (2.6% opens, 49 of that edition's 61 spam reports) before the next send.
  3. The plan ceiling. Whether the 700,000 limit counts active subscribers only, and for this publication only (the workspace holds three others). This decides whether inactive status is enough or deletion is ever needed.
  4. IP and warming. Shared pool or dedicated IP, and why a domain in use since at least December 2025 still shows warming_up.
  5. How the halves and waves were formed. Posts addressed to one 666,924-member segment reached 125,521, 252,169, 324,457 and 324,369 recipients with no exclusion on the post record. If the halves were not random, the Gmail gap between them means less.
  6. Why Gmail opened the first send of the day at 5% to 9% and the next at 13% to 25%, three times out of three. Not determinable from beehiiv.
  7. Why the June sunset flow was switched off after 8 days, and how many people it deactivated (the step counts are ambiguous: 11 completed, 2,678 recorded as unsubscribed).
  8. What validation the tools apply before calling the beehiiv API, which governs 97% of sign-ups, and whether imported records enter the New Subscribers drip.
  9. Whether beehiiv's built-in welcome email is on, and whether a reader can change the two audience flags on the manage-preferences page.
  10. The From and reply-to address of each post. The sending subdomain has no mail server, so replies to the From address have nowhere to go unless a different reply-to is set.
  11. What beehiiv's per-send suppressions (9,271 to 18,460 on large sends) consist of.
