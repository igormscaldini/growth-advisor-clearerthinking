Do Mental Health Diagnoses Actually Hold Up?

Subtitle: What 143 symptoms and traits in about 1,000 people suggest about diagnoses.

Key points

  • Diagnostic cutoffs, such as five of nine symptoms for major depression, are inevitably arbitrary.
  • In one of the author's studies, depression and anxiety scores correlated at about 0.8.
  • A general "p factor" may reflect overall vulnerability to many mental health problems.
  • Dimensional models like HiTOP and diagnostic categories may each be useful in different ways.

By Spencer Greenberg

Chances are that you or someone you care about has been diagnosed with a mental health disorder, whether that is major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, PTSD, or OCD. These labels affect a great deal, from what insurance pays for to how we think about ourselves and each other.

So it is worth asking a controversial question: do these categories actually hold up? Here are some of the strongest critiques of them, and where I come down.

Where the Categories Come From

In the United States, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) defines what meets the criteria for a mental health disorder and what does not. At the end of the day, those decisions are made by committees of experts. Over time, the committees have relied more and more on studies, which is great, but they have also made some serious mistakes in the past.

The first edition, published in 1952, listed homosexuality as a type of "sexual deviation" under the heading "sociopathic personality disturbance." The first two editions also had no diagnosis for autism and classified it under childhood schizophrenia; autism did not become its own diagnosis until 1980.

The system has improved a lot since then, but some people argue that even the current DSM has fundamental errors baked into it.

Where Do You Draw the Line?

The most basic critique is about boundaries. How do we decide who is depressed and who just has a bit of a low mood?

To meet the DSM-5 criteria for major depressive disorder, you need at least five of nine listed symptoms during the same two-week period, and at least one of them has to be depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure.

Now imagine a person with a very depressed mood and a total lack of interest and pleasure in life, but none of the other symptoms, such as fatigue, sleep disturbances, or suicidal thoughts. Most of us would think that person is depressed. Yet they do not meet the criteria.

The problem is not that the criteria are wrong per se. Wherever you draw the line, it is going to be arbitrary, and someone who really seems depressed will land just on the other side of it. That might keep their insurance from covering their treatment.

It also matters what counts as meeting a single criterion. Criterion four is "insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day." Different clinicians can disagree about what qualifies, so you might get a different diagnosis depending on whom you see.

The Overlap Between Depression and Anxiety

A weirder observation calls the whole idea of separate categories into question. I first noticed it in our own research, where we measured people's depression and anxiety levels with two different scales. For anxiety we used the GAD-7, which is very often used in research.

Much to my shock, the correlation between depression scores and anxiety scores was about 0.8. To put that in context, it is about the same as the correlation between the GAD-7 and simply asking people, "What percentage of the time are you stressed or anxious?" (As I have written elsewhere, depression and anxiety are highly related but do not seem to be the same thing.)

Is this a symptom of something bigger? We collected data on roughly a thousand people, who reported their mental health symptoms and traits, and looked at the correlations between all pairs of 143 of them.

Fewer than 5 percent of the pairs had a negative correlation. Less than 18 percent had close to no correlation. The rest, the large majority, were positively correlated. This suggests that having almost any mental health condition increases your chances of having others.

One Number Behind It All?

That pattern raises an intriguing possibility: a single latent factor underneath mental health, one number that represents a person's propensity to get a wide range of mental health challenges.

This is known as the p factor, or psychopathology factor. The researchers who coined the term, in a 2014 paper, compared it to the g factor of general intelligence.

When we applied factor analysis to our data, a p factor was there. It represented about 25 percent of the variability in people's responses across all 143 questions. Intriguingly, it did not do a very good job of representing narcissism or sociopathy, which appeared in a second factor. A third, smaller factor was related to social difficulties.

How to interpret the p factor is debated. Is it genetic? Environmental? Something about the way people answer questions? Realistically, I think it is probably a combination of all three.

Either way, the p factor presents a challenge to diagnosing by category. If people who meet the criteria for one diagnosis may also meet the criteria for another, which category do you put them in? And do the categories even explain what is going on?

A version of that question, whether mental health is best modeled by categories or factors, is one of the example questions in the Unanswered Questions in Psychology Prize, a project my team and I are running to identify the most important open questions in psychology. Anyone can submit a question, and academic psychologists (current or former) will vote on the winners.

A Map Built From the Data

A number of researchers got together and took a different approach: rather than trying to force categories, look at the empirical data and try to structure mental health the way it actually is. The result is HiTOP, the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology.

At the top is the general factor. Below it are six broad spectra:

  • Internalizing: distress that is directed inward, as in anxiety and depression.
  • Disinhibited externalizing: includes substance use problems.
  • Antagonistic externalizing: includes narcissistic traits.
  • Thought disorder: includes hallucinations and delusions.
  • Detachment: includes little interest or pleasure in life, and social withdrawal.
  • Somatoform: physical symptoms related to our psychology, such as some digestion issues, pain issues, or fatigue (included on a provisional basis).

Because the system is hierarchical, you can keep subdividing these into narrower and narrower traits. And it does not require you to put people in buckets: you can say that this person is high on these seven traits and that person is high on these five.

So Should We Drop the Categories?

It's complicated. HiTOP lets you talk about people in a really nuanced way: for the person from earlier, we can simply talk about the two depression criteria they do meet.

But categories can be useful at times. Human brains tend to think in categories, which can oversimplify but also helps us reason about situations. We also sometimes have to make binary decisions, like whether a person gets a medication or not. And some people fit the buckets quite well: getting to know one person with narcissistic personality disorder can tell you quite a bit about other people with the disorder.

My own belief, and it is anecdotal, is that really extreme forms of these traits tend to make people behave more like a category. People who are a bit narcissistic don't have much in common. In people who are incredibly narcissistic, the trait drives much of their decision-making and behavior, and they start acting much more alike.

I also think there are nuances that factor models can easily miss. I know quite a few people who are sociopaths, meaning that they completely lack empathy and guilt. Talking to them made me realize they have things in common that you would not expect from that alone. Their relationship to fear, for example, seems to be different from other people's.

My view is that both systems are very useful, in somewhat different ways. HiTOP is a great model that helps improve on what we are already doing, but that does not mean we should throw away all categories. It is particularly important to notice when someone does not quite fit a category, and not to force it on them.

As for what the right set of categories or factors actually is, that is one for the Unanswered Questions list.
