# Psychology Today post: writing kit

Prepared 2026-11-05 for whoever writes the post (Igor or Spencer). Source: Spencer Greenberg's video "Are Mental Illnesses Fake?" (published 2026-10-20, 12:26, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9wq49DUENHw). The script is in `transcript.txt` beside this file.

This is notes, facts and sources, not prose. Psychology Today's Editorial Process page (https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/docs/editorial-process) says: "Contributors are not permitted to use generative-AI to author their text in whole or in part, nor are they permitted to alter the tone and voice of their copy using AI-generated edits or suggestions. [...] authors must certify that they have read our editorial guidelines and that the piece aligns with all guidelines including the use of generative AI. Tools including AI are permitted for research and brainstorming." So the sentences have to be a person's. Spencer's script is his own wording and is the natural starting text.

## 1. Format to hit

Psychology Today's detailed posting rules sit behind the contributor login. "Measured" below means counted on live posts on 2026-11-05; "GAP" is the author guide of another Psychology Today blog (https://ourgap.org/gap-blog-guidelines), a secondary source.

- Title: 60 characters at most (GAP). Measured on 20 Essential Reads: 27 to 58 characters, all title case.
- Subtitle: 80 characters at most (GAP). Measured: 19 of 20 end with a period or question mark.
- Key points: 3 or 4, each 110 characters at most (GAP; matches every post measured). Full declarative sentences, third person.
- Length: 400 to 1,300 words (GAP). Measured: Essential Reads median about 990 words; this blog's 13 posts run 641 to 1,727, median about 1,040. The script is 2,255 words, so about half has to go.
- Subheads: 16 of 20 Essential Reads use 2 to 6 H2 subheads.
- Sources: "References are provided in the body of the text or appended as footnotes" (Editorial Process). In practice: an inline link per factual claim plus a short References block in APA style. This blog's two 2026 posts list 3 and 4 references.
- Voice: first person is normal (15 of 20 Essential Reads use "I" or "my"). The two 2026 posts on this blog open with an italic in-body byline ("By Spencer Greenberg, Ph.D. and Jeremy Stevenson").
- Language: none of the 20 Essential Reads contains profanity, so the script's "kind of bullshit" needs replacing.
- Promotion: the Terms of Use bar "advertisements or solicitations of business" outside the author profile and content that includes "unauthorized commercial activities, such as contests, sweepstakes, advertising". Cut the script's ending (Unique Traits test, the book, subscribe). See section 4 for what this means for the prize.

## 2. What editors promote there

Evidence:
- Two of the 20 current Essential Reads are critical takes on diagnosis: "The 'Culting' of Mental Health Diagnosis" and "Anxiety Is Often a Symptom, Not a Diagnosis". The DSM topic page features "What the DSM Can't See" and "When No Psychiatric Diagnosis Seems to Fit".
- Essential Reads titles: 1 of 20 is a question, none starts with a number, 3 start with "Why" and 3 with "How". Numbers appear in subtitles ("8 truths", "seven strategies").
- 5 of the 6 Most Popular posts were also editor-promoted; 3 of the 6 report a new study.
- A long-time contributor (Craig Malkin, 2016 interview): posts that take off are "easy to read, rarely over 800 words", with a title that answers or asks a question the reader already has.
- This blog has never covered diagnostic categories, the p factor or HiTOP. Its closest post is "Why You Shouldn't Always Trust Personality Tests" (spectra versus types).

Inference (mine, from a one-day sample): a short declarative or "why" title, one clear and slightly contrarian claim, the practical payoff in the subtitle, about 1,000 words. A sceptical but fair piece on diagnostic categories fits what is being featured now. The original data (the 0.8 correlation, the 143 symptoms) is the element other posts on the topic do not have, so it belongs near the top.

## 3. Outline, section by section

Times are the video's chapters. "Fix" items come from a fact check against primary sources; the script is accurate in substance everywhere except the HiTOP count.

**A. Hook (0:00 to 0:34, target about 80 words)**
- Keep: almost everyone knows someone with a diagnosis; diagnoses decide what insurance pays for and how we see ourselves and each other.
- Replace: "kind of bullshit".
- Supported: US insurers generally require a diagnosis code to reimburse treatment. The codes are ICD-10-CM codes, which the DSM lists beside each diagnosis, so avoid "DSM code" (APA DSM FAQ; Ruggero et al., 2019).
- Not checked: the claim about criminal sentencing. Cut it or find a source.

**B. Where the categories come from (0:34 to 1:45, about 120 words)**
- Keep: criteria are drafted by expert committees; evidence plays a growing role; two historical errors.
- Precise facts: the first DSM (1952) listed homosexuality as a type of "sexual deviation" under "sociopathic personality disturbance" (DSM-I, pp. 38-39). DSM-II (1968) gave it its own code. The APA voted in December 1973 to stop classifying homosexuality itself as a disorder; the last homosexuality-specific diagnosis went in 1987 (Drescher, 2015).
- Precise facts: DSM-I and DSM-II had no autism diagnosis; such children were classified under "Schizophrenic reaction, childhood type", then "Schizophrenia, childhood type". "Infantile autism" became its own diagnosis in DSM-III in 1980 (Rosen, Lord & Volkmar, 2021).
- Fix: field trials and literature reviews start with DSM-III (1980); the first two editions had none.

**C. Where do you draw the line? (1:45 to 3:24, about 200 words)**
- Keep: the five-of-nine rule, the person with severe low mood and no pleasure who still falls short, the insurance consequence, and "what counts as a sleep disturbance?".
- Precise facts: DSM-5 requires five or more of nine symptoms "during the same 2-week period", at least one being depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure. Criterion 4 is "Insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day". DSM-5-TR (2022) kept the nine symptoms and the threshold.
- Worth adding, because clinicians read this site: that person would not meet major depressive disorder, but the DSM has a fallback, "other specified depressive disorder" (depressive episode with insufficient symptoms).
- Optional number for "it depends who you see": in the DSM-5 field trials, two clinicians interviewing the same patient on separate occasions agreed at kappa 0.28 for major depressive disorder and 0.20 for generalized anxiety disorder (Regier et al., 2013). Say "separate interviews", not "the same interview".

**D. The overlap nobody expects (3:24 to 5:25, about 220 words)**
- Keep: depression and anxiety scores correlated at about 0.8, roughly the same as the anxiety scale against simply asking how often you feel anxious; the 143-symptom result (fewer than 5% of pairs negatively correlated, less than 18% near zero).
- Cut: the walk through the chart's colours and the "question 30, question 40" example, unless the chart itself goes in the post.
- Facts from Spencer's own essays: the depression scale was the PHQ-9; the written figure is r = 0.82 in 870 US participants recruited through Positly ("What is a large correlation?", spencergreenberg.com, written 2018). "About 0.8" is fine.
- Keep his own caveat in view: the same essays say depression and anxiety "don't, by any means, seem to be 'the same thing'".
- Gap: the 143-symptom dataset and the "percentage of the time anxious" comparison appear only in the video. No write-up exists on clearerthinking.org, spencergreenberg.com or the podcast. Get the sample size, recruitment and date from Spencer and describe it as his team's own data.
- The script's step from "symptoms correlate" to "one condition raises the chance of another" is backed by outside data: 45% of US adults with any disorder in a year met criteria for two or more (Kessler et al., 2005).

**E. One number behind it all? (5:25 to 7:24, about 200 words)**
- Keep: the p factor as overall vulnerability; the comparison with the g factor of intelligence (drop the pointer to the IQ video); in his data it captured about 25% of the variability, missed narcissism and sociopathy (second factor), with a smaller third factor for social difficulties; nobody agrees what it is.
- Precise facts: the term was coined by Caspi et al. (2014), who draw the g comparison themselves and warn that the correlations "do not prove the existence of a unitary g-like causal factor".
- The script's three explanations (genes, early environment, the way people answer questions) all appear in Caspi & Moffitt (2018): twin studies, childhood maltreatment, and "it is possible that p is not a substantive dimension but merely a response style". The literature adds two more: symptoms causing one another, and p as a plain index of severity.

**F. A map drawn from data (7:24 to 9:08, about 130 words)**
- Fix (the one real error): HiTOP has six spectra, not five. Internalizing; disinhibited externalizing (substance use problems); antagonistic externalizing (narcissistic traits); thought disorder (hallucinations, delusions); detachment (social withdrawal, low interest or pleasure); somatoform, included "on a provisional basis" (Kotov et al., 2017). Antisocial behaviour loads on both externalizing spectra. Leave paranoia out of the thought-disorder examples; HiTOP papers place it across several spectra.
- Soften "completely data-driven": HiTOP is a consortium of researchers building "an empirically driven classification system" from which symptoms occur together. The contrast with the DSM is the evidence base, not the absence of committees.
- Compress the spectra into one sentence or a short list.

**G. So should we drop the categories? (9:08 to 11:19, about 200 words)**
- Keep: dimensions describe individuals with more nuance; categories match how people think and some decisions are yes or no; his three personal observations (extreme cases resemble each other, the people he knows who lack empathy and guilt relate differently to fear, the person with narcissistic personality disorder and high empathy).
- Label these as personal observations. On his podcast (episode 298) he called the first one "totally anecdotal, I don't have the data to back this up".
- My note, not checked in this research: "sociopath" is not a DSM diagnosis, which a clinical reader may point out.

**H. Close (11:19 to end, about 60 words)**
- Keep: both systems are useful in different ways; do not force a category on someone who does not fit it.
- Cut: all three promotions.

## 4. The two mentions of the Unanswered Questions project

Facts that can be stated (from the live form and its rules):
- One of the form's own example questions is this post's topic: "Is mental health best modeled by categories or factors, and what is the right set of categories or factors to carve the reality of mental health differences at the joints?"
- The project aims to identify the most important open questions in psychology. Anyone may submit; current and former academic psychologists vote on the winners. Its rules say it is inspired by the Millennium Problems in mathematics.
- Link: https://programs.clearerthinking.org/unanswered-questions-in-psychology

Where they fit:
1. End of section D or start of G, where the script asks whether the categories explain what is going on. The mention is that this exact question is on a list of candidate open questions for the field.
2. Section E or the close, where the script admits nobody knows what the p factor is. The mention is that questions like this one are what the project is collecting, with the link.

Cautions:
- Disclose that it is Spencer's own project ("a project I run"). That is honest and reads less like a pitch.
- The Terms of Use bar "contests" and solicitations in posts. Describe the project and link it; leave out the cash amount and any "enter now" line, or expect an editor to cut it.
- Leave out the deadline: the post will outlive it.

## 5. Title, subtitle and key-point ideas (brainstorm only; the final wording is the author's)

Titles (all within 60 characters):
- Are Mental Health Diagnoses Real Categories?
- What the DSM's Categories Miss About Mental Health
- Why Depression and Anxiety Are So Hard to Tell Apart
- Is One Hidden Factor Behind Most Mental Health Problems?
- The Trouble With Sorting Mental Health Into Boxes
- Where Does 'Just Sad' End and Depression Begin?

Subtitles (all within 80 characters):
- Why the line between disorder and no disorder is blurrier than it looks.
- What 143 symptoms in about 1,000 people reveal about diagnostic labels.
- Symptoms overlap far more than diagnostic labels suggest. What does that mean?

Key-point angles (each fits 110 characters as worded):
- Meeting a diagnosis often comes down to a cutoff, such as five of nine symptoms for major depression.
- Scores on common depression and anxiety scales can correlate at about 0.8.
- A general 'p factor' may capture overall vulnerability to many kinds of mental health problems.
- Dimensional models such as HiTOP and traditional categories are each useful in different ways.
