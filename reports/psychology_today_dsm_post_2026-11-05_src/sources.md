# Source evidence for the Psychology Today post

Collected 2026-11-05 by two research passes (primary sources for the video's factual claims; Spencer Greenberg's own published material). Quotes are verbatim unless marked. There are no slides: the evidence is the transcript (`transcript.txt`) plus the sources below. The post is written in Spencer Greenberg's first person, so his statements in the transcript are the author's own.

## The video

"Are Mental Illnesses Fake?", Spencer Greenberg's YouTube channel, published 2026-10-20, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9wq49DUENHw. Its description lists as sources: DSM-I (1952), DSM-5 (2013), Spitzer et al. 2006 (GAD-7), Caspi et al. 2014 (p factor), Kotov et al. 2017 (HiTOP), and "Anxiety and Depression Correlation (GAD-7): Personality Map." The 143-symptom dataset has no published write-up anywhere; the video is its only source.

## DSM-I (1952) on homosexuality

American Psychiatric Association (1952), Diagnostic and Statistical Manual: Mental Disorders, pp. 38-39, under "000-x60 Sociopathic personality disturbance", entry "000-x63 Sexual deviation": "This diagnosis is reserved for deviant sexuality which is not symptomatic of more extensive syndromes, such as schizophrenic and obsessional reactions. [...] The diagnosis will specify the type of the pathologic behavior, such as homosexuality, transvestism, pedophilia, fetishism and sexual sadism (including rape, sexual assault, mutilation)." https://archive.org/details/dsm-1

## Autism in the early editions

DSM-I (1952), p. 28, "000-x28 Schizophrenic reaction, childhood type": "Psychotic reactions in children, manifesting primarily autism, will be classified here." DSM-II (1968), p. 35, "295.8 Schizophrenia, childhood type": "The condition may be manifested by autistic, atypical, and withdrawn behavior". Neither edition had a diagnosis called autism.

Rosen, Lord & Volkmar (2021), https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-021-04904-1: "autism was included for the first time in DSM-III" (1980), as "infantile autism".

## DSM committees and evidence

DSM-I and DSM-II title pages: "Prepared by The Committee on Nomenclature and Statistics of the American Psychiatric Association". Surís, Holliday & North (2016): "Field trials were conducted with each successive version of the DSMs beginning with DSM-III."

## DSM-5 major depressive disorder

Criterion A (verbatim reproduction in Wachowska & Gałecki, 2021, Table 1): "Five (or more) of the following symptoms have been present during the same 2-week period and represent a change from previous functioning; at least one of the symptoms is either (1) depressed mood or (2) loss of interest or pleasure." Criterion 4: "Insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day." Criterion 6: "Fatigue or loss of energy nearly every day." Criterion 9 concerns recurrent thoughts of death and suicidal ideation.

A person with only the first two symptoms is below the five required. The DSM has a category "Other specified depressive disorder"; a peer-reviewed guideline (Avasthi & Grover, 2018) paraphrases one of its examples as depressed affect plus at least one other symptom for two weeks. Correction after review: the APA DSM-5-TR fact sheet on this category only confirms that it exists and does NOT contain the phrase "depressive episode with insufficient symptoms", so the post no longer mentions the fallback label.

## DSM-5 field trials

Regier et al. (2013), https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2012.12070999, abstract: the trials measured "the degree to which two clinicians could independently agree on the presence or absence of selected DSM-5 diagnoses when the same patient was interviewed on separate occasions"; "five diagnoses were in the very good range(kappa=0.60–0.79), nine in the good range(kappa=0.40–0.59), six in the questionable range (kappa = 0.20–0.39)". The full text was not read; the diagnosis-level figure comes from papers citing it: "the pooled intraclass Kappa was 0.28 (95%CI 0.20-0.35) at the adult field trial sites" for major depressive disorder (Liu & Jiang, 2016); "generalized anxiety disorder (κ = 0.20) [...] and major depression (κ = 0.28)" (Nemeroff et al., 2013).

## GAD-7

Spitzer, Kroenke, Williams & Löwe (2006), https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.10.1092: "The GAD-7 is a valid and efficient tool for screening for GAD and assessing its severity in clinical practice and research." Jordan et al. (2017): "one of the most frequently used diagnostic self-report scales".

## Spencer Greenberg's written statements on depression and anxiety

"What's the link between depression and anxiety?" (spencergreenberg.com, written 2021-01-10): "in one of my studies, when I correlated PHQ-9 depression scale scores with GAD-7 anxiety scale scores, I found that r = 0.82". Same essay: "So yes, depression and anxiety are highly related, but they don't, by any means, seem to be 'the same thing.'"

"What is a large correlation?" (spencergreenberg.com, written 2018-01-13): "0.82: depression symptom score (PHQ9) vs. anxiety symptom score (GAD7)", from "870 people in the United States". It is not confirmed that the video's 0.8 comes from this same study.

## The p factor

Caspi et al. (2014), https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702613497473: "Here we have coined the term p factor to represent this dimension of General Psychopathology, in recognition that it conceptually parallels a dimension already familiar in behavioral science: the g factor, or general intelligence." Also: the correlations "do not prove the existence of a unitary g-like causal factor."

Caspi & Moffitt (2018): "it is possible that p is not a substantive dimension but merely a response style"; "Twin studies reveal common genetic influences on negative emotionality and a general factor of psychopathology"; childhood maltreatment "influences broad, general factors [...] common to multiple different types of disorders".

## HiTOP

Kotov et al. (2017), https://doi.org/10.1037/abn0000258: the consortium "brings together a group of clinical researchers who aim to develop an empirically driven classification system based on advances in quantitative research on the organization of psychopathology." "Altogether, six spectra were included in the HiTOP model: internalizing (or negative affectivity), thought disorder (or psychoticism), disinhibited externalizing, antagonistic externalizing, detachment, and somatoform". "Thus, the somatoform spectrum has been included in the HiTOP model on a provisional basis." "Superspectra are extremely broad dimensions comprised of multiple spectra, such as a general factor of psychopathology that represents the liability shared by all mental disorders."

Substance use belongs to the disinhibited externalizing spectrum; the antagonistic spectrum covers "narcissistic, histrionic, paranoid, and borderline personality pathology"; antisocial behaviour loads on both (Kotov et al., 2017). Detachment: "detachment (i.e., social withdrawal)"; Kotov et al. (2020) list "anhedonia (deficits in positive emotions and energy)" and "social withdrawal". Thought disorder "ranges from normal reality testing, to maladaptive trait psychoticism, to hallucinations and delusions" (Kotov et al., 2020). Paranoid traits sit across several spectra, so the post leaves paranoia out.

Known discrepancy with the transcript: the video names five spectra with a single "externalizing"; the source has six. The post follows the source.

## Spencer's own caveat on the "extremes behave like categories" remark

Clearer Thinking podcast, episode 298 (January 2026), Spencer: "what I've observed, and this is totally anecdotal, I don't have the data to back this up, is that people who are a bit depressed don't have very much in common. People who are a bit narcissistic don't have very much in common. But the most depressed people [...] seem to have a lot in common".
