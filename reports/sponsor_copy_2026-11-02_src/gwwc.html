Week of November 19: the pledge, by way of the pond

Newsletter box

🌊 You would ruin your shoes to save a drowning child. $7 protects one from malaria for a season.

Peter Singer's thought experiment is more than fifty years old and still uncomfortable. You walk past a shallow pond where a small child is drowning. Wading in would wreck your expensive shoes. Everyone agrees you wade in. Then the question: why does the answer change when the child is far away and the cost is $7, which is what Malaria Consortium needs to protect one child through a malaria season?

Agreeing with the argument and acting on it are different things, and Giving What We Can's 10% Pledge exists for the gap between them: a public commitment to give at least 10% of your income to the organisations you believe can most effectively use it to improve the lives of others. More than 11,000 people in 121 countries have taken it, and pledgers have given more than $390 million.

Igor, who runs growth at Clearer Thinking, took it this spring after a one-year Trial Pledge, and is glad he did. If 10% sounds like a lot, that is what the Trial Pledge is for: any percentage, even 1%, for as little as six months.

Take the 10% Pledge, or start with a Trial Pledge.

Week of December 8: finding the charities that do 100 times more

Newsletter box

🔍 Most of us pick a charity the way we pick a restaurant. The difference in results can be 100 to 1.

A friend mentions it, the website looks trustworthy, the cause feels right. Fine for dinner. For a charity it can cost most of the good your money could do, because on Giving What We Can's own comparison, the best charities do around 100 times more per dollar than a typical one.

Finding them is a research job, and Giving What We Can does not do it alone. They do not rate charities themselves; they rely on independent, impact-focused evaluators such as GiveWell, publish which evaluators they trust and why, and assess those evaluators' methods first. On the 2026 list that comes out of it, $6 buys a malaria net that protects two people for two years, $7 protects a child through a malaria season, and $2 supplies a year of vitamin A, which prevents a deficiency behind many child deaths.

If you would rather not pick, their cause-area funds split a gift across the top options according to the evaluators' research. The list takes about ten minutes. Read it before you give this year.

Week of December 29: the resolution that holds

Newsletter box

🔸 A New Year's resolution with 11,397 witnesses

Resolutions fail quietly: nobody notices when you stop. The 10% Pledge is built differently. You commit in public to giving at least 10% of your income to the organisations you believe can most effectively use it, you join 11,397 people who made the same commitment, and each year you report what you gave. That report is the part most resolutions lack.

It also answers a familiar December objection: "I will give more when I earn more." If you earn about $65,000 a year after tax, you are already in the richest 1% of people alive. The pledge turns that fact into a standing decision instead of a December one.

Igor, who runs growth at Clearer Thinking, took it this spring and is glad he did. Not ready for 10%? The Trial Pledge lets you pick any percentage, even 1%, for six months to five years.

Take the 10% Pledge, or start with a Trial Pledge.
