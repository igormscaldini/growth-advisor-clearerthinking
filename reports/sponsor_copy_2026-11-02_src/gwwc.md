## Week of November 19: the pledge, by way of the pond

### Newsletter box

🌊 **You would wreck your shoes to pull a child from a pond. Protecting a child through a malaria season costs $7.**

Peter Singer's thought experiment is more than fifty years old and still uncomfortable. You walk past a shallow pond where a small child is drowning. Wading in would ruin your shoes. Almost nobody says no. Then the question: why does the answer change when the child is far away and what you can do costs $7, the amount Malaria Consortium needs to protect one child through a malaria season?

Agreeing with the argument and acting on it are different things, and the 10% Pledge was built for the gap between them. It is a public commitment to give at least 10% of your income to the organisations you believe can most effectively use it to improve the lives of others. More than 11,000 people in 121 countries have taken it, and pledgers have given more than $390 million.

Igor, who runs growth at Clearer Thinking, took it this spring after a one-year Trial Pledge, and is glad he did. If 10% sounds like a lot, the Trial Pledge is the on-ramp: any percentage, even 1%, for as little as six months.

[Take the 10% Pledge, or start with a Trial Pledge](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/pledge).

## Week of December 8: finding the charities that do 100 times more

### Newsletter box

🔍 **A lot of us pick a charity the way we pick a restaurant. The difference in results can be 100 to 1.**

A friend mentions it, the website looks trustworthy, the cause feels right. Fine for dinner. For a charity it can cost most of the good your money could do, because on Giving What We Can's own comparison, the best charities do around 100 times more per dollar than others.

Finding them is a research job, and Giving What We Can does not evaluate individual charities itself. It relies on independent, impact-focused evaluators (GiveWell among them), says which ones it trusts and why, and assesses their methods first.

The 2026 list that comes out of it has nine charities and funds. On it, $6 buys a malaria net that protects two people for two years, $7 protects a child through a malaria season, and $2 supplies a year of vitamin A, which prevents a deficiency behind many child deaths. If you would rather not pick, the cause-area funds divide a gift among the top options according to the evaluators' research. Read the list before you give this year.

[See the best charities to donate to in 2026](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/best-charities-to-donate-to-2026) or [give through a fund](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/donate/organizations).

## Week of December 22: the last-week gift

### Newsletter box

⏳ **About 14,000 children will die today of diseases we know how to prevent or cure. If you give once more this year, here is where it goes furthest.**

Yes, this is also a last-minute appeal. The difference is that it points at a list built by independent evaluators rather than at one charity. The 14,000 figure is from Giving What We Can's 2026 charity list, and so is this example from it. New Incentives gives a child every routine vaccination for $146, by paying cash incentives at government clinics in northern Nigeria after each shot.

If your year-end gift usually goes to whoever asked last, the list is a better default. It covers global health, animal welfare and global catastrophic risks, and Giving What We Can vets the evaluators behind every entry before relying on them.

Out of time? The cause-area funds take one gift and spread it over the top charities in a cause, so a single decision is enough, and it is done before December 31.

[Give through a fund before the year ends](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/donate/organizations) or [see the best charities of 2026](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/best-charities-to-donate-to-2026).

## Week of December 29: the resolution that holds

### Newsletter box

🎯 **A New Year's resolution with [11,397] witnesses**

[Max: the number is the live pledger count on the day; if the 12,000 goal is reached by then, use this headline instead: "The 12,000th person just made this resolution in public. Here is why it holds."]

Resolutions fail quietly, because nobody notices when you stop. The 10% Pledge is built the other way round. You commit in public to giving at least 10% of your income to the charities you think do the most good, you join [11,397] people who made the same commitment, and each year you report what you gave. That report is the part most resolutions lack.

It also answers a familiar December objection, "I will give more when I earn more". If you earn about $65,000 a year after tax, you are already in the richest 1% of people alive. The pledge turns that fact into a standing decision instead of a December one.

Igor from our team took the pledge this spring, after a year on a Trial Pledge, and is glad he did. The Trial Pledge is still the way in if 10% feels like too much: any percentage, even 1%, for six months to five years.

[Take the 10% Pledge, or start with a Trial Pledge](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/pledge).
